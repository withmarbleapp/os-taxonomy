## Read-aloud study: where water reaches us

Water can be present naturally in rivers, lakes and underground. People can build wells to reach underground water, reservoirs to store water and pipes to carry it. A tap is an outlet, not a machine that creates water. A storage tank holds water that arrived from somewhere else. Natural water and built systems can be parts of one supply route.

In our original model Town L, water comes from a lake, is taken to a treatment facility, stored in a tower and carried through pipes to a school tap. In our original model Home W, a constructed well reaches groundwater and a pump and pipe carry it to the home. In model School R, rain is collected from a roof into a tank; the model specifies treatment before drinking. These are supplied example routes, not descriptions of the learner's own actual supply.

Do not infer that all lake water, all groundwater or all collected rain is safe to drink. The EPA source-water explanation distinguishes source protection, treatment and protection during distribution. This activity identifies sources and systems; it does not test drinking-water safety or give treatment instructions.

## Independent E2.2 check

After teaching, put the route descriptions and labels away. Give the learner these three neutral prompts, using words, simple drawings or movable cards:

1. A school is connected to a lake supply through a treatment facility, tower and pipes. Name the natural water source and the built places from which people can obtain or store water. Explain what each does. Does the tap create the water?
2. A home obtains water through a well. Point to the underground water and the constructed well separately. Explain why one case can contain both natural water and a built source/access structure.
3. A school collects rain from a roof in a tank. Name the natural input and built collection/storage. If the tank is empty and no rain or delivery replenishes it, can opening its outlet supply an unlimited amount? Explain.

Require an independent example of another natural water location and another built water access/storage case. Accept a correctly described built reservoir, well, tank or piped supply; do not insist every real reservoir is exclusively natural or exclusively constructed. Ask the learner to justify the described case rather than classify from its name alone.

Full source performance requires independently identifying multiple natural sources and multiple built access/storage/supply cases, explaining at least one natural-to-built route and responding correctly to the tap/tank countercases. Naming a river and a road or merely sorting generic natural/built features is insufficient. Feedback may teach afterward; supplied causal answers during the check do not count as independent performance.

## Read-aloud study: using water responsibly

Homes use water for different purposes, such as drinking, food preparation, washing and toilets. Some activities require safe water and should continue. A responsible-use plan reduces avoidable running or loss while meeting the household's needs. It does not mean drinking less than needed, skipping washing or assuming all homes have the same fixtures.

A City of Toronto household resource recommends not leaving a tap running continuously while brushing teeth and using an appropriately full dishwasher/laundry load; it also identifies leaks as a cause of water loss. We adopt only those general examples. We do not assign its estimated volumes to an unmeasured household, require new appliances or ask children to inspect or repair plumbing.

Example model: Rowan leaves the tap running throughout brushing. Another household already turns it off during brushing but has noticed a dripping tap. A fitting plan differs: Rowan can avoid unnecessary running; the second household should tell the responsible adult about the leak. Repeating “turn off the tap” without looking at actual use misses the second problem.

## Independent E1.2 plan and transfer

Ask the learner to assess their own completed record: Which uses meet needs? Where could avoidable running/loss be reduced? What supports that choice? Require a plan with an actual chosen use, the needed information/materials, an ordered action, who is responsible, the benefit, and how to check whether the action is suitable. The learner explains the first step and why it fits the assessment. If the household already uses water carefully, they can plan to maintain that practice and check an identified uncertainty; they must not invent waste to satisfy a rubric.

For transfer, supply a changed household in which the learner's first selected action is already done, while a different actual-use problem is declared. Ask whether the same recommendation still addresses that problem and what should change. A plan copied from a supplied conservation list, unrelated to the record, does not pass.

Full source performance requires an independent assessment of actual personal and household uses, a fitting ordered plan grounded in that assessment, preservation of necessary uses and a justified changed-case response. No observed implementation, efficacy result, household saving or whole-topic mastery is claimed by authoring this protocol.

## World-community preparation: E1.3 remains Partial

UNICEF's dated 25 August 2025 account describes these particular communities, not every household in a country or present-day guarantees:

- In Khormaloq village, Afghanistan, families had relied on a distant stream described as unsafe; a piped solar-powered supply improved access near homes.
- In the São Francisco Macuxi community in Brazil, river collection involved travel and seasonal difficulties. The community identified a well used by the new supply system; less collection time was a reported benefit.
- At selected schools in Điện Biên, Viet Nam, rainwater collection and treatment supplied water for daily use.

Ask learners to locate the three countries on an ordinary world map, identify the differing water inputs and built systems, and explain a reported consequence for daily life. Compare physical water being present with safe drinking water being available where needed. Keep these dated cases attributed; no country-wide deficit label, current universal guarantee or numerical development ranking follows.

This is useful accessible content but does not yet justify changing E1.3 to Full: neither water-location knowledge alone nor generic fairness establishes the whole access-and-community comparison.
