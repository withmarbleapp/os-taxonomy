## Teaching and assessment use

These are original supplied-source studies and fictional decision models, not reports of local observations. Read each study card with the learner, explain unfamiliar words and discuss a worked example. Use ordinary oral explanations, drawings or writing to make reasoning accessible. Do not supply the assessed decision or reorder the learner’s model for them.
Then remove the ordered study sheet. Give the learner the separate jumbled stage tokens or decision cards listed below. The learner constructs and explains the relation; an adult may read an unordered token verbatim without giving its position. After an error, teach using the study material and later reassess with the changed case. Merely repeating a teacher’s answer is insufficient.
Assess each source requirement separately. Whole-topic checks and hard prerequisites remain separate: success here does not award all life-cycle vocabulary, all insect transformations, plant diagrams, human life stages or the complete organism–habitat repertoire. No field collection, handling wildlife, water access, hazardous-product handling or practical habitat alteration is required.

## Study: One animal action can help and create a conflict

A beaver changes its stream habitat by building a dam; the new wetland can provide habitat for other animals.
In this model, one valued tree beside a public path has been chewed by a beaver. A responsible adult has identified a possible safety/property problem, and children stay away from the tree and water.
The responsible site team can choose selective tree protection that keeps a beaver from reaching that particular trunk while leaving other appropriate habitat and food available. The child does not fit the protection.
Model alternative R removes all trees around the pond; it would also remove habitat and food. Model alternative P protects the identified valued tree while retaining other suitable vegetation.
No flooding is occurring in this first model. A separate changed case introduces backed-up water threatening an access route; protecting a tree trunk does not control that water level. Qualified adults assess the changed flood risk and site-appropriate response; this case does not prescribe a device or dam removal.

## Study: A bee visiting flowers

A bee visits flowers for food. During visits, pollen can stick to its body and be carried to another flower. This helps many flowering plants produce seeds and fruits. People and other animals can benefit from those plants. Some plants use wind or water or can pollinate themselves; it is not true that every plant needs bees.
Our fictional garden contains flowers that need visiting animal pollinators. The model changes only the availability of these visits, keeping water, light and suitable growing conditions the same. If visits are missing, fewer flowers in this model produce fruit. We have not measured a real garden or claimed that every crop disappears.

NPS About Pollinators, main explanatory body read 2026-11-12; facts limited to pollen transfer and plant reproduction. Fictional garden stipulation is an analytic model, not an NPS experiment.

## Study: Two different human activities

The fictional public path needs a safe accessible route. Route A would remove the particular pond-edge vegetation that the supplied animal case uses for shelter/cover. Route B follows an existing cleared area and, in this declared model, can meet access needs while retaining that vegetation.
People require access and safety; the selected pond animals require their declared shelter and food conditions. The model does not claim all real routes or restoration projects have these exact effects.
Qualified adults choose and carry out any route work; the child explains the comparison, does not enter a habitat or move animals.
A second, distinct activity in this fictional community is dumping unwanted paint or oil onto land where rainwater can carry it into the pond. These products can make the water dangerous for fish. This is pollution, not a way to dispose of waste.
The model offers an adult-managed appropriate collection route for unwanted products. Keeping them out of the pond reduces this stated pollution risk, but does not make all waste disappear or guarantee that every environmental problem is solved. Children explain the choices and tell a responsible adult; they do not touch, pour or collect the products.

Route/vegetation effects are explicitly supplied model conditions. ECCC pollution education main body 26–37,56–58,73–74 read 2026-11-12 supports the distinct pollution chain; no local disposal rule or clinical mechanism is inferred.

## Study: Monarch butterfly (Danaus plexippus), insect

A female adult lays eggs on milkweed. An egg hatches into a caterpillar, the larva of the same individual.
The caterpillar crawls and eats milkweed leaves as it grows. Its striped body looks different from the winged adult.
The larva forms a chrysalis; this is the pupa stage, in which the insect transforms rather than seeking and eating leaves. A chrysalis is a pupa, not a new species; a cocoon is a covering some insects make, not a separate stage required here.
An adult butterfly emerges with wings. It flies to flowers and drinks nectar. Reproductive adults may produce the next generation of eggs.
An individual eventually dies; some individuals die before reproducing. Reproduction starts a new individuals cycle; death does not turn the same individual back into an egg.

## Study: American bullfrog (museum source uses Rana catesbeiana), amphibian

A female lays eggs in water. A hatchling is a tadpole of the same species; it has a tail, breathes with gills and swims.
Bullfrog tadpoles eat algae, organic material and insect larvae. They grow and develop legs.
The froglet transition has developing legs and a shrinking tail; the tail is absorbed rather than simply falling off. There is no pupa stage.
The adult has legs and no tadpole tail, breathes with lungs and moist skin, and can use its legs to swim or hop. Reproductive adults can produce the next generation of eggs.
Individuals eventually die and some do so before reproducing; offspring, not a rejuvenated dead adult, begin the next cycle.

## Study: Mallard (Anas platyrhynchos), bird

An adult female lays eggs in a nest. The young hatch as down-covered ducklings, not tadpoles or larvae with a pupa stage.
Ducklings grow. Their later wing feathers and flight ability develop over time; newly hatched ducklings cannot yet fly.
A selected healthy, flight-capable older mallard can swim and fly. An adult female can lay eggs for the next generation. Not every individual reproduces or survives to adulthood.
The chosen duckling and adult both have a beak and two legs; their size and plumage differ. The adult does not become a different species.
Adults eventually die. Offspring start a new cycle; individual death is not hatching again.

## Study: Eastern cottontail (Sylvilagus floridanus), mammal

A female gives birth to young in a nest and nurses them with milk. This selected mammal does not hatch as a caterpillar, tadpole or bird from an externally laid egg.
Newborns cannot yet see; their eyes open as they grow. Young later leave the nest and stop depending on milk. Older young and adults eat plant material.
In the selected comparison, the young cottontail is past its newborn stage, has open eyes, has fur and is leaving the nest to forage. Both this fur-covered young animal and the fur-covered adult have two ears and four legs, while the adult is larger. They remain the same species.
Some mature individuals reproduce and have a new generation of young. Individuals eventually die, and some die before reproducing.
Reproduction links the generations; death does not restart the same individual.

## Independent B1.1 protocol


materials:
- Unordered case labels: beaver dam, new wetland, other-animal habitat, chewed valued tree, tree safety/property conflict, bee flower visit, moved pollen, seeds/fruits, selective tree protection, remove all trees, changed flooding.

prompt:
Build two animal-action chains, using the beaver and the bee. Explain effects on the environment and on people. In the beaver tree case compare removing all trees with protecting the identified tree: choose and justify a way to reduce the negative effect while retaining suitable habitat. Then explain what changes when backed-up water threatens the path.

requiredEvidence:
- Distinct animal→environment→people/environment explanations from both animals.
- Negative impact and cause-specific mitigation, with habitat consequence and limit.
- Flooding is a different problem; protecting a trunk does not control water level, so qualified site assessment is needed.

insufficient:
- Animals are good/bad labels alone.
- Teacher-selected solution repeated without a cause chain.
- A claimed guarantee that one intervention solves every beaver conflict.

## Independent B2 protocol


prompt:
Make your own four-animal comparison after studying the cards. Explain how each grows and changes and how their physical characteristics distinguish them. Show one shared pattern of growth and next-generation reproduction, one stable and one changing physical feature, and compare a major transformation with more gradual growth. Connect a supplied stage-dependent movement or feeding behaviour with its body/stage. Explain why a growing animal remains the same species. Then reject the proposed claim that every animal needs a pupa stage.
