## Prevention: original model and fresh decision

Teaching scene, explicitly fictional: Jo sees an unfamiliar dog beside a school gate. Another pupil suggests approaching it for a photograph. The adult explains the checked precautions: give the dog space; avoid rushing towards it or disturbing it when eating or sleeping; stay calm rather than run or shout if it approaches; seek the responsible adult's help. Signs such as growling or a stiff body are reasons to give space, not a reliable test of disease or proof an animal will behave predictably. This lesson requires no animal, approach, petting, feeding, chase or physical attack rehearsal.

Ask Jo's pupil reader to explain a safer next step and why the photograph is not worth approaching. Use a picture or spoken description; no eye-contact or emotional-display norm is scored.

Fresh independent scene: a familiar dog is eating near a route a group planned to use. Ask what the pupil should avoid doing and which actual school adult can help with a different approved route. Accepted reasoning keeps distance, avoids disturbance and gets adult assistance; familiarity is not a guarantee. Children do not capture, move or manage the dog. A generic “be careful” without an action/help route is insufficient.

## Reporting and first-aid awareness

The first-aid examples below concern dog bites/scratches. Other injuries need their appropriate school health response; this is not a universal protocol for every animal injury.

Original brief pupil message: “If a dog bites or scratches you, tell a responsible adult immediately. Do not hide it or wait for symptoms. The adult should arrange prompt washing with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes and urgent medical assessment. Washing helps, but does not replace professional care.” The school adult models the actual reporting route and health response; pupils do not diagnose an animal, select medicines or decide vaccination needs. Harm is not the child's fault, including if the child froze or could not get away.

Teaching scene, fictional: Rin has a small scratch after contact with a dog and says, “It hardly shows, so I will wait.” Ask the learner to identify what to report, whom to tell and why prompt adult/professional help matters even without a dramatic wound. No wound, exposure or frightening personal disclosure is staged. Use clean uninjured props or an illustration only; the lesson is awareness, not practising treatment on another child.

Fresh independent scene: a dog bite is reported while the usual class teacher is unavailable. Using the school's actual approved backup adult route, pupil explains whom to tell next, the washing/medical-help sequence, and why neither concealment nor washing alone is sufficient. The key is prompt reporting, appropriate adult-led first aid and professional assessment; there is no compulsory medical vocabulary or treatment-plan quiz. In an actual incident, responsible adults follow the real emergency/health procedure rather than finish this classroom assessment.

## Independent awareness task and retained boundaries

Remove the worked answers and give a new safe described animal encounter plus a separate reported dog scratch/bite scenario. Require an explained prevention decision, the actual primary/backup reporting route, and recognition that adult-led first aid and urgent professional care are both needed. Offer accessible oral, signed, visual or written responses as appropriate. Record support and actual understanding; a memorised poster alone does not establish application.

Existing complete safety/help-seeking claims and their broader criteria remain independent. This bounded source task awards no entire topic or clinical competence automatically. Institutional staffing, fencing, waste arrangements, inspections, animal management and any official action report remain real adult obligations; this resource neither performs nor certifies them. No messages, accounts, official reports or health interventions are initiated by this authoring work.
