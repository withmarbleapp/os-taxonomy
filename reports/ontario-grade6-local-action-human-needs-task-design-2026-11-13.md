## E1.1 original factual resource cards

These short source-based cards are the delivered teaching input. They are not answers to the independent assessment. Use plain diagrams and the stated facts; all original drawings can be reproduced from the instructions below. Reading may be supported; the learner supplies causal explanation. No clinician advice, private emotion report, actual astronaut simulation or child hazard exposure is required.

**A. Inside and outside a protected habitat.** Outside the ISS cabin, a person lacks the ordinary breathable, pressurised environment supplied inside. NASA describes spacecraft systems that control pressure and oxygen, circulate/clean cabin air and recover water. These are different functions: adding oxygen alone does not provide every environmental condition. [NASA ECLSS actual main269–284](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/environmental-control-and-life-support-systems-eclss/). Draw an original two-panel cabin/outside boundary; inside label air/pressure/ventilation/water controls, outside no ordinary breathable cabin atmosphere. Do not label the drawing an image measured by NASA.

**B. Orbital microgravity and body loading.** Crew and spacecraft fall around Earth together; gravity still acts. Reduced ordinary weight-bearing loading can weaken muscles/bones over time. Specialised resistance equipment/harnesses help provide loading; they do not replace air, food, water or social support. [NASA K–4 actual275–283](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-k-4/) and [CSA actual35–61,78–129](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/astronauts/living-in-space/physical-activity-in-space.asp). Draw a floating cabin figure and a separately labelled resistance-device diagram, without a child exercise prescription, numerical load, disease forecast or “there is no gravity” claim. NASA's loose opening weak-gravity phrase is not adopted as the explanation of ISS floating.

**C. Food, water and practical preparation.** People still need food for energy and water. CSA describes nutritious, compact, shelf-stable and suitably packaged food; packages/utensils are managed so food does not float into equipment. Shared meals can also support social connection. [CSA selected actual38–73,110–137](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/astronauts/living-in-space/eating-in-space.asp). Draw a labelled food pouch, drinking pouch and secured storage; no calories, menu prescription, packaging experiment, tasting requirement or future-mission claim.

**D. Sleep and rest in an unusual environment.** Sleep remains a body need. A regular routine and control of disturbing light/noise help sleep in orbit; sleeping bags are secured and suitable ventilation matters. [CSA actual main24–78](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/astronauts/living-in-space/sleeping-in-space.asp). Draw a secured sleeping bag with simple dark/light and quiet/noise controls; do not suggest a child can test space sleep by restricting their own sleep or breathing. No sleep-duration advice is imported.

**E. Confinement, relationships and emotional comfort.** Long periods away from family in a small shared place can be difficult. CSA's AtHomeInSpace research describes personal items and a shared crew culture; connections with family and colleagues matter. Familiar messages, private communication and shared voluntary activities can help, without guaranteeing every person feels the same. [CSA actual main26–60](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/at-home-in-space.asp). Draw a small shared cabin, private message area and optional shared activity as distinct supports. They are possible supports, not clinical treatment or compulsory happiness.

**F. Outside work requires a different system.** A spacewalking suit supplies pressure, oxygen, cooling and communication; it is not merely warm clothing. Cabin protection does not automatically follow a person outside. [NASA actual268–296](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/spacewalk-spacesuit-basics/). Draw a suit linked to pressure/air/cooling/radio labels; no construction of a real suit, temperature extremes exercise, oxygen/pressure apparatus or current future-suit schedule. Keep a planned spacewalk distinct from an unprotected cabin person.

## E1.1 teaching: condition, impact, need and response

Supply the six actual cards, blank original cabin/suit diagrams and a four-column sheet: **condition**, **human impact/need**, **response**, **limits/other need still unmet**. Teacher models one different example: a noisy cabin can disturb rest; a protected quiet period can help sleep but does not supply oxygen or family contact. Ask why a response fits that condition, not just whether it sounds impressive.

Learner then uses cards to explain environment/pressure/air and water provision, reduced loading and exercise support, food/energy and packaging, sleep/routine and social/emotional connections. They may identify a support serving more than one need (a shared meal provides food and connection) if the explanation is sound. Needs are not artificially disjoint categories; social means relationships/cooperation, emotional includes comfort/privacy/familiarity, physiological includes bodily survival and functioning. Do not require private self-disclosure, a cheerful reaction, diagnosis or an assumption that one leisure activity suits all crew members.

Contrast a cabin with ample food but poor ventilation, a well-equipped cabin with no protected rest, and a crew with adequate physical supplies but no family/crew connection. Pupil explains why one good provision does not meet every need and suggests a specific response with a limit. Correct the three near misses: floating means no gravity; food replaces exercise/air; entertainment guarantees wellbeing. Discussion of astronaut exercise is analysis of a science accommodation, not a PE performance duty.
