# Grade 1 Canadian money: source-bound historical review

This original teaching and assessment design reviews the already reported Full
G1 F1 and F1.1 records. It adds no topic and claims no learner result. It does not
recertify Grade 2/3 currency ranges or the entire Kindergarten mathematics goal.

The official [Mathematics 2020, January 2021 document](https://assets-us-01.kc-usercontent.com/fbd574c4-da36-0066-a0c5-849ffb2de96e/3ed76499-9df4-4524-8cc2-3c0010a51811/The%20Ontario%20Curriculum%20Grades%201%E2%80%938%20-%20Mathematics%2C%202020%20%28January%202021%29.pdf), printed page 140, requires
Canadian denomination recognition and value comparison within the Grade 1
coin/note limits. A generic jar of frequently used coins can omit the 50-cent
denomination. The complete existing Coins & Notes claim is retained unchanged;
its local-currency inventory, feature-based recognition, value ordering,
same-value designs and equivalent combinations remain separately assessed.

## Actual teaching materials

Use the issuer's [printable play money](https://www.bankofcanadamuseum.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/printable-play-money-monnaie-de-jeu-imprimable.pdf)
or supervised appropriate real money. All five PDF pages were rendered and
visually inspected on 2026-11-12. Page 1 supplies 5-, 10- and 25-cent pieces;
page 2 supplies one- and two-dollar pieces; pages 3–5 include the 5-, 10-, 20-
and 50-dollar notes. Do not include the 100-dollar illustration in the Grade 1
source assessment. These are deliberately marked play-money representations,
not exact reproductions of every current design, texture or security feature.

That PDF contains neither a penny nor a 50-cent piece. Add the Royal Canadian
Mint's [50-cent reference](https://www.mint.ca/en/discover/canadian-circulation/50-cents)
and its [actual illustrated example](https://www.mint.ca/globalassets/learn/discover/canadian-circulation/50-cents/heroblock_discover_canadian-circ_50-cents_lg.jpg).
The illustration was inspected: the denomination and coat-of-arms design are
visible, but the photograph is cropped. Do not use its apparent screen size to
compare real coin diameters. The Mint explains that this denomination is used
infrequently; unfamiliarity from ordinary pocket change is not evidence that it
does not exist. No purchase or acquisition of collectible money is needed.

For one cent use an explicitly marked play token and the Mint's
[penny reference](https://www.mint.ca/en/discover/canadian-circulation/1-cent).
Do not say pennies are currently being produced or distributed. The penny is
not required as a real object for this task. For other accurate designs use the
Mint's [circulation reference](https://www.mint.ca/en/discover/canadian-circulation)
and the Bank's [note reference](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/banknotes/).
The adult checks the selected denomination before delivery; a changed monarch,
orientation or artwork is not itself a changed value. No security inspection or
authenticity judgment is an assessment requirement.

Issuer illustrations stay at their source links; none is copied into this
repository. Adult-prepared materials may be displayed rather than printed.

## Read-aloud teaching sequence and key

Introduce cents and dollars as named units: one dollar is 100 cents. Use the
visible unit/denomination and other available features to recognise each item;
do not teach colour, silhouette or picture as an infallible single cue. State
these values while modelling, then remove the teaching value list:

| Representation | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Marked play penny | 1 cent |
| Nickel | 5 cents |
| Dime | 10 cents |
| Quarter | 25 cents |
| Fifty-cent piece | 50 cents |
| Loonie | 1 dollar |
| Toonie | 2 dollars |
| Notes within this source task | 5, 10, 20 and 50 dollars |

Accept an accurate denomination such as “five-cent coin”; memorising the
nickname “nickel” is not a separate source requirement.

Give the learner time to inspect and sort. Read labels aloud during teaching
when needed; record that support separately from the independent check. Compare
the nickel and dime using actual objects or an appropriately scaled
representation: the dime can be physically smaller but worth more. A zoomed
screen image is not a measurement of the real object. Contrast the number of
pieces with total value using one 10-cent piece and two 5-cent pieces.

Teach with one worked equivalence, then assess different examples. Provide the
100-cent unit relation when needed; independent written arithmetic to 100 is not
an added Grade 1 money gate. Keep 5 cents and 5 dollars explicitly different.
An isolated calculation slip and misunderstanding a denomination are different
observations. Use fictional money only; family income, spending and possession
of cash are irrelevant.

## Independent F1.1 denomination and comparison task

Put away the teacher's value table. Present the selected coin/note images or
objects in a changed order and orientation, including the separate 50-cent
example, the marked 1-cent representation and every listed note through $50.
Ask the learner to identify the denomination, state its value and point to a
feature supporting the recognition. Reading the denomination printed on the
object is legitimate; reading a teacher's supplied answer list is not.

Ask for comparisons across the inventory: nickel/dime, quarter/fifty-cent,
one-dollar/two-dollar, five-dollar/ten-dollar and twenty-dollar/fifty-dollar.
Require a value reason, not physical-size or piece-count ranking. Then compare
5 cents with 5 dollars using their different units. A correct ordered list of
coin names without recognising the actual representations is insufficient.

Change a design or show the other side of an accurate same-denomination item;
the learner must check its denomination rather than apply a remembered
colour/position answer. If the chosen view supplies insufficient information,
asking to see the other side is appropriate. Before delivery, the adult checks
that each assessment image is legible and its teacher key matches the actual
view. The source assessment requires the full stated inventory, including 50
cents, not a random subset that happens to be available.

## Independent F1 overall and complete-claim checks

For the overall, give a fresh mixed set and ask the learner to organise it by
value, explain the units, compare two differently composed amounts and explain
why changing the number or physical size of pieces need not change the amount.
Use two quarters versus one fifty-cent piece, two $5 notes versus one $10 note,
and one $20 note versus two $10 notes. The learner constructs or explains the
equal-value groupings; do not provide a completed matching sheet as evidence.
Then change one quarter to a dime: the two-coin group is now worth less than 50
cents. Change one $5 note to $10: that group is now worth more than one $10 note.
Ask for the reason rather than a memorised pair.

The complete Coins & Notes criterion also requires two different combinations
for a selected same amount. Invite the learner to make and explain these with
the available representations, then use a changed amount. Correct examples are
two 5-cent pieces OR one 10-cent piece for ten cents; two quarters OR one
fifty-cent piece for fifty cents; two $5 notes OR one $10 note for ten dollars.
The teacher key does not mean the teacher builds the learner's answer.

Record an incorrect value, a unit confusion, an unsupported physical-size rule,
and failure to recognise a changed design separately. Teach the missing
distinction and later use a fresh case. Source-only success does not certify
all other country configurations of the local-currency claim.

The unchanged hard prerequisite **What Money Is** (`mt_SsS7GptD_o`) separately
requires explaining payment for needs/wants, an exchange/barter example and
different money/payment forms. Denomination recognition does not certify that
whole claim. The British **Coin Values** relation is soft; recognition of UK
pence/pounds is not required before the Canadian task. Neither edge is changed.

## Review and preservation boundary

This is a fresh source-authoring review of two historical Full records, with
zero net closure count. The existing Ontario 6–9 context and its Grade 1–3
specific-code associations are preserved. The proposed F1 overall contribution
is a component from the same unchanged Coins & Notes claim in that context;
the independent overall task above is required, not inferred by counting child
links. All other ages, claims, contexts, prerequisites and research flags remain
unchanged. The complete currency inventory/physical feature tasks are an
implementable design, not observed learner mastery or a validated intervention.

Factual adoption is limited to issuer denominations, the shown representations,
50-cent availability and the penny-production boundary. No prices, collector
valuation, household financial recommendation, policy rule or saving/spending
lesson is adopted from the linked sites. The Museum's Grade 5 lesson was used
only to locate its printable resource; its Grade 5 activities are not imported.
