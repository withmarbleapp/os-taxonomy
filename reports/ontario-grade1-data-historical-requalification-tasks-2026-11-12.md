## Original physical materials and actual versus model evidence

Prepare two opaque labelled boxes, A and B, each containing eight clean large shape cards. Teacher setup only: A has five circles, two triangles and one square; B has three circles, three triangles and two squares. Keep B's composition hidden before the prediction. Make shapes equally sized and accessible with raised outlines/labels if needed. These are genuine classroom material populations when actually made and counted; they are not invented pupil votes or neighbourhood observations. A supplied diagram can rehearse the task but does not certify physical collection.

Provide blank record sheets with columns category/tally/total, a graph mat with a shared baseline and equal-size spaces, blank pictograph paper, a single-unit bar-grid sheet, an unmarked category-order strip and blank title/source/label boxes. Add a separate single-attribute colour/size set for transfer. The pupil builds and labels displays; completed graph answers remain teacher keys until after an independent attempt.

For an actual interview use one neutral question with declared response categories, such as which of three supplied paper display symbols a partner would choose. Obtain actual voluntary answers at delivery, record one response per person without names and include a decline/no-answer category or narrow the stated responding population honestly. Do not substitute a teacher's fiction for performed interviews. No family, address, identity or emotional disclosure is needed. The authoring protocol is complete without contacting anyone now.

For chance work supply an inspectable fair coin, fair die labelled1–6 and bags with known safe counters; use large suitable pieces. The teacher verifies equipment/conditions and distinguishes random draws from deliberately showing that a possible outcome exists. Do not fake random results, manufacture a 10/10 coin split or guarantee a particular outcome on a small run.

## D1.1: sorting one attribute and explaining the rule

Pupil sorts A by shape, describes the rule and explains why a card belongs. Then re-sort a different set by colour only or size only; do not mix attributes halfway. Count each category and say how the total remains eight after rearranging. A new oddly positioned card tests whether orientation is confused with category. Source credit needs a single coherent attribute, not all geometry vocabulary.

For the complete Sorting 2D/3D Shapes contributor separately provide 2D shapes with different side counts and actual 3D solids with flat/curved surfaces, require two sorts and explanations; an eight-card shape-colour sort does not demonstrate its entire geometric repertoire.

## D1.2: actual observation, experiment and interview collection

The pupil chooses a single piece of information and a question that a small declared population can answer. For observation ask which shape is most frequent in A, inspect each card exactly once, record by a chosen method and organise an actual tally table with totals. Groups of five tallies are taught, not prefilled as observed work. For experiment choose the die outcome after each actual roll and record the genuine sequence; for interview use the neutral actual-partner question above. Keep the populations/sources distinct. Do not describe all three channels as completed because one supplied table was read.

Whole Collecting Data adds categorical AND discrete numerical cases and spreadsheet entry. Separately count a non-sensitive attribute, such as number of sides on each actual polygon card, record the frequency table, then enter the same category/count columns in an actual accessible spreadsheet and check they match. Actual tool/input/access preflight remains required; paper source success is not spreadsheet evidence. Whole Recording Data also retains actual labelled observational drawings and measurements where selected, tied to the original question.

## D1.3: both concrete graph and pictograph

From the actual A tally, pupil constructs a concrete graph with one object/token for each card, consistent category baseline and spaces, and supplies a title, category labels and actual source such as “Box A shape cards counted today”. Independently draw a pictograph with one icon per card and explicit one-icon/one-card key. Check counts against the table. A larger decorative circle does not count as more cards. Change one actual card, then update both displays and their totals rather than reproduce the old key.

Whole Picture & Bar Graphs additionally requires an independently drawn single-unit bar graph with labelled axes and up-to-four-category work; supply a fresh four-category model with counts4/3/2/1, label it teacher-created, then ask total10, first-and-third total6, how many more first than fourth3 and the remaining total after removing the third category8. Supplied numbers are a declared dataset, not actual interview results. Source concrete/pictograph production remains separately assessed; a bar graph does not replace it.

## D1.4: decreasing frequencies across all three representations

Pupil orders A categories by frequency: circles5,triangles2,squares1. Give an independently prepared equivalent tally/concrete/pictograph representation and check order again; change the B data so circles and triangles tie at3 and squares2. Accept equal-frequency categories in either order while explaining the tie. Category names/visual size are not the sorting criterion. A changed display uses different icon placement but preserves counts; a common start and one-to-one correspondence matter.

## D1.5: questions, conclusions, arguments and decisions

Ask pupil to generate and answer a direct count question, compare two categories and find the total, then decide which shape-card supply needs more stock for a declared construction plan using the observed A demand. They must explain which data supports that decision and what the dataset cannot establish. If the task is actual interview preferences, use those actual responses rather than treating object frequency as preferences. A fresh question asks whether this small box proves every box or pupil group has the same distribution; key no, identify another population/data collection needed. Pupil asks a follow-up question from an unexpected category result, not only repeats the teacher's conclusion.

Whole Statistical Investigation retains a declared question, actual collection, suitable representation including the bar-graph case and two independently composed/dictated evidence-based concluding sentences. Copying a supplied argument is insufficient. No raw private pupil dataset is committed.

## D2.1: likelihood, prediction and informed decision

First use the source terms impossible/possible/certain on inspectable devices: a green counter from an all-red bag is impossible; red is certain; red from a mixed red/blue bag is possible but not certain. Predict before drawing and explain a decision, such as selecting the all-red bag if the stated aim is to be sure of red. Then change the bag to include a blue counter and ask what word/decision changes. Wanting red is not evidence.

For complete Likelihood Language retain all five graded words in independently justified use: seven red/three blue means red likely-not-certain, blue unlikely-not-impossible, absent green impossible, red-or-blue certain; five red/five blue gives even chance. An ordinary fair1–6die has three even and three odd faces, so even is even chance;7 is impossible. A place/date-conditioned everyday event and its opposite preserve context; never teach universal July snow or sunrise assumptions. A pupil must use/justify words, not merely point to a supplied label.

Whole Simple Chance Experiments additionally retains actual twenty coin flips, thirty die rolls and a counter-draw case, recorded outcomes and comparison with the expected pattern. Where its full assessment asks for a larger pooled set, use actual recorded100–500trials (or a transparently attributed supplied pooled dataset for interpretation), compare observed proportions with the stated equal-chance model, and explain independence rather than assuming a recent streak makes the next outcome due. A supplied pooled dataset does not establish the learner performed those trials. This broader saved9–10guide repertoire is retained as a distinct whole-topic assessment; it is not an implicit Grade1 numerical-proportion requirement or evidence for a new6–7whole offer. These are observations only if performed, and uneven short-run counts do not prove the device unfair. Source likelihood language alone does not award the complete experimental claim. There is no forced statistical calculation gate in the Grade1 source task.

## D2.2: predict AND test two distinct equal-size populations

After actually counting A, pupil predicts BEFORE opening B whether B's same categories will have the same frequencies and gives a reason. Both boxes have eight items but composition is not inferred from equal size. Open B, collect its data by the same categories, compare category-by-category and explicitly check the prediction. A key: circles5vs3,triangles2vs3,squares1vs2, totals8and8. Explain why the totals can match while category counts differ.

Use fresh C/D sets with equal totals but different declared compositions and require another prediction/test. Changing only the arrangement of A is not a different population; re-running draws from the same bag does not supply this exact source operation. For a source-allowed people-group version, use actual or explicitly supplied model class counts of the same group size and retain a genuine category-wise comparison; fictional classroom votes are labelled models, not performed interviews. Whole Comparing Two Groups retains a prediction before collection/check, actual second-group counts, explicit confirmation/revision and an explanation of variation. Different-size groups must not be silently treated as this same-size task.
