## N1 — The red bag

I had a red bag. Sam had a book.
“Can it fit?” I said.
Sam put the book in my bag. The bag shut, but the strap did not sit flat.
“Stop,” said Sam. “Let us fix it.”
I put the book on the mat. We set a small box by the shelf. Sam made a sign. I drew a book on it.
Then Jo came in. Jo did not know where to put a book. I held up the sign.
“Here,” I said.
Jo grinned. Our box had a job.

Teacher guidance: first-person narrator is the child saying “I”; Sam is another character. Important sequence: bag problem, book removed, box/sign solution, Jo uses sign. Infer the bag is an unsuitable storage solution from the strap/detail, not from the colour red. “Our box had a job” refers to its use. A different supported inference is acceptable with a detail. Fresh prompts: retell from Sam's viewpoint; predict before the last four lines then check; explain how a drawn book helps Jo. Do not supply the solution before the learner's own account.

## N2 — The cart in the rain

I wanted our reading cart beside the door. Everyone would see it there. Before lunch, I made a bright sign: “Find a book! Find a friend!”
My partner, Dev, looked at the empty corner by the window. “Could the cart go there instead?”
“But people come in at the door,” I said.
Rain began to rattle on the roof. Soon, coats crowded the doorway. Dev held a damp sleeve away from the books. I moved my sign out of the splash.
At lunch, we asked our teacher which routes had to remain clear. Then we tried the corner. The cart fitted beside the wall, and we put an arrow at the door.
After lunch, a visitor followed the arrow. “I found it,” she said.
I still liked my bright sign. Now I liked Dev's idea too.

Teacher guidance: the first-person narrator changes an opinion after evidence; Dev's suggestion and the rainy doorway motivate the move. The arrow directs, rather than proves every visitor will notice it. Infer that visibility and a clear/dry route are different considerations. Compare N1's box and N2's cart using evidence; a first-person version does not tell everything Dev privately thought. Fresh task: narrate Dev's account, identify one known event and one thought the original never establishes.

## N3 — A place for the quiet sign

Mira had painted a huge word on a sheet of card: QUIET. She put it above the reading mat and stood back. The word looked powerful. It did not make the room quiet.
Near the window, two children were rehearsing a story. One spoke while the other made the sound of rain with paper. At the mat, Jo covered one ear and closed a book.
“They need a smaller sign,” Mira decided.
Mr Bell did not agree or disagree. “What is the problem you want the sign to solve?” he asked.
Mira watched again. The storytellers were not reading the sign. They were watching each other. Jo wanted to read, but the paper rain kept catching Jo's attention.
Mira drew a plan. She marked a reading space and a rehearsal space. “Could we try different times?” she asked.
The group tested a short rehearsal period followed by quiet reading. Jo opened the book again. During rehearsal, Mira joined in with a soft paper rustle.
Later she wrote a new sign: “Check the plan. Some times are for stories; some times are for quiet reading.” Below it she put two simple symbols.
The sign was longer. The problem was clearer.

Teacher guidance: third-person narration chiefly follows Mira's observations/decisions. Main idea: investigate a need and revise a communication solution, rather than assume a bigger command fixes everything. Distinguish observed ear-covering/closed book from a diagnosis or universal sensory preference. Cause/effect links involve competing activities and timing; sequence alone does not establish every cause. Fresh task: propose another supported title, summarize with the main idea and relevant details, and explain how a first-person Jo narrator might change the information/voice.

## N4 — Two plans, one opening

While Mira planned the reading corner, Noah planned its opening celebration.
Mira drew two routes on squared paper. One crossed the place where the club stored its sports equipment. The other stayed beside the wall. She wanted visitors to find the books without squeezing between bags. She asked the adult responsible for the room to check both routes; her drawing alone could not approve a layout.
Across the room, Noah practised a welcome song. The club had invented its own custom: at each term's opening, an older member offered a new member a blank bookmark. The new member could decorate it or leave it plain. This term, Noah also wanted a short dance that the group had created together. Watching was welcome; nobody had to sing or dance.
Mira heard the rhythm and imagined all the chairs filling the route she had drawn. Noah saw her face and stopped. “You think the song is too loud?” he asked.
“I am thinking about the chairs,” Mira said.
They compared their plans. Noah moved the performance area on the drawing; Mira added a clear place for people who wished to watch from farther away. The adult checked the actual arrangement later.
On opening day, the bookmark box went missing. Noah retraced his steps to the equipment shelf. Mira found it behind her folded route plan. They laughed, then checked the labels together.
The song began. A new member chose a plain bookmark and sat near the edge. Noah smiled and carried on. Mira watched the route: it stayed open.

Teacher guidance: two interleaved plot strands are Mira's route planning and Noah's celebration planning; misunderstanding brings them together, and the missing box is another connected event. Sequence each strand separately, then explain their intersection and cause/effect with evidence. Third-person narration provides both strands; characters cannot infer another's thoughts from a face alone. Norms/values/artifact/sport/music examples are the FICTIONAL club's optional participation, welcome, bookmarks, equipment and song/dance, not an account of a real people. Explain what the text supports and what would need real community sources. Compare those represented elements with a checked authentic creator text, retaining its own context.

## N5 — The label that needed a label

Mira had promised that the reading corner would be ready on Friday. She had also promised herself that it would look spectacular. On Monday, she covered a page with curling letters and tiny golden stars. “No one can miss this,” she said.
Meanwhile, Noah collected responses to a club question. In the fictional records, eight respondents chose quiet reading and ten chose group stories. He wrote the question and the total beside the two counts. Then he discovered a draft poster that showed only the ten story responses. Its heading said, “EVERYONE WANTS STORIES.”
“It is not everyone's answer,” he said.
Mira barely looked up. “Could we fix it after the sign?”
On Tuesday, their teacher asked a visitor to try the sign. The visitor liked the stars but could not find the opening time in the small print. Mira's pride folded like a wet paper boat. She made the time larger and removed some decoration.
Noah brought the response sheet. Mira read it properly. Together they changed the heading to “Our club chose two activities.” They kept both counts and added the question. The records were still only a small model club response, not a survey of every reader in a town.
On Thursday, they labelled the box for spare labels. Noah printed LABELS. Mira added a label explaining that LABELS meant the box of labels. Then they wondered whether that label needed a label too. The joke lasted longer than the printing.
On Friday, the plainest line was the most useful: “Quiet reading and group stories have different times. Check the plan.” Mira had kept one golden star. It pointed to the time.

Teacher guidance: track Mira's design/revision plot and Noah's evidence/heading plot, then explain how the strands combine. The wet-paper-boat image suggests deflated pride; it does not establish a literal wet boat. The repeated labels make recursive humour. Use local inference (why enlarge the time?) and global inference (how does Mira's view of useful design change?). The “everyone” heading is contradicted by its own model source; do not invent a real survey. Independent transfer: compare a fresh “all readers” caption against the stated counts and limits, and rewrite a short passage from Noah's first-person perspective without pretending to know unstated thoughts.

## N6 — A welcome with room to change

I had a million ideas for our hall's opening. A tower of books! A curtain of paper stars! A speech so grand that even the chairs would applaud! Then Asha asked where she could sit if she wanted to hear the welcome without joining the dance.
I pointed at the front. She pointed at the plan. The front was beside a speaker, and the route to it crossed the dancers' space. My million ideas became one useful question: who had our plan forgotten?
At the same time, Noah was preparing a report. His first draft called the new room “perfect for everyone”. The building in our fictional story had a wide, step-free doorway, a sheltered entrance and large windows. Those features mattered, but they did not prove that every person could use every activity comfortably. Noah asked what the report's evidence actually showed.
We revised together. I kept the opening ritual our fictional club had created: an older member welcomes a new member, then offers a bookmark. Taking one is optional. We described its purpose as welcome, not a test of belonging. Our shared dance had a seated part and a standing part; a person could also watch. These are this fictional club's invented practices, not instructions for performing a real community's ceremony.
Noah added subheadings about the building, activities and remaining questions. Under a drawing of the entrance, he wrote, “A step-free route is one feature; ask users what else they need.” He removed a photograph-sized blank box: there was no photograph to put in it. A labelled drawing was more honest.
Before the opening, I whispered, “How do I break the ice?”
“Start with hello,” Asha said. “Leave the frozen puddle alone.”
We laughed. A quiet greeting did what my grand speech had not yet done: it started a conversation.
Afterwards, Noah asked for feedback. One visitor wanted the timetable in another format. We recorded the request without posting the person's name. “Finished,” I said, then crossed it out. “Ready for another revision.”

Teacher guidance: first-person narration is bounded by what the narrator observes/learns; Noah's parallel report is another strand reported through that voice. “A million ideas” is hyperbole; “break the ice” is an idiom meaning ease the start of social interaction; Asha's literal puddle reply creates humour. Investigate represented values, an opening ritual, the building's architecture, visual design/art and dance using actual textual evidence; none is a claim about real Indigenous/other named cultural practices. Compare with the authentic particular creator materials and a voluntary, checked local example. Main conclusion: inclusion is an ongoing evidence-informed process, not guaranteed by one building feature or a fictional narrator's claim. Fresh second-person narrative conversion: “You reach the entrance carrying your plan. You expect the front seat to suit everyone. Then you notice where the speaker stands.” Explain how direct address changes reader involvement and what it does not prove about a real reader.

## I1 — Model staff brief

Original FICTIONAL document, dated as a teaching model; no real school policy.

**Purpose:** help people find and return reading materials.
**Need:** the room's adult must approve any arrangement. A clear walking route must stay clear. Labels should say what is in each box. A picture can help, but it must agree with the words.
**Requested output:** a readable label, a short timetable and a message explaining how to use them.
**What this brief does not tell you:** exact room dimensions, every user's access needs, a spending budget or permission to publish anyone's image.

Use this as one source in the model inquiry. For an actual design task replace it with a real approved brief and record who supplied it, when, the actual need and constraints. No real need or permission is inferred from the model.

## I2 — Model club note and table

Original FICTIONAL document from the story's club, not actual pupil data.

Question: “Which activity would you like at one of our club sessions?” Eighteen model responses were recorded; each chose one option.

| Choice | Model responses |
| --- | ---: |
| Quiet reading | 8 |
| Group stories | 10 |

The note recommends different times for the two activities. It does not say that a person who chose stories dislikes quiet reading, that all eighteen attended, or that the club represents a town's entire population.

## I3 — Model visitor request

Original FICTIONAL account, not the lived-experience testimony of a real person.

“I can read the large heading, but the small time is hard for me to find. An icon can help me locate the timetable. Please keep the written time as well. Colour alone is not enough to tell me which activity is happening. I would like a choice of places to sit.”

The speaker states one person's preferences. Other people may need different arrangements. Compare this request with I1 and I2: these documents answer different questions. For a real project gather an actual willing user's or responsible adult's feedback in an accessible way; do not assign these fictional words to that person.

## I4 — Model advertising panel

Original FICTIONAL advertisement, not a real offer or a link to a service.

**SUPER SPARKLE SIGNS!**
“Make our sign and EVERY child will read twice as fast! Our club's ten story votes prove it. Get a free badge: send your full name, home address and a photograph. Everybody agrees!”

Small print: No reading-speed test, creator credentials, consent or complete response record is supplied. There is no real address or form to contact. Learners analyze the provided panel; they do not follow its request.

Answer guidance: no supplied evidence establishes faster reading or universal agreement. Ten story votes concern activity preference, not reading speed; I2 also records eight quiet-reading choices. Identify commercial/persuasive purpose, urgency/capitalization and missing evidence; explain privacy and permission decisions. Rewrite the message honestly for a stated audience rather than treat a polished graphic as credible. Unknown facts remain unknown.

## O1 — Oral instructions and non-verbal contrast

Teacher reads with the text hidden: “Before choosing a book, look at its label. Put a returned book in the return box, not straight on a shelf. If a label is unclear, ask before moving it. After checking the timetable, choose a place that suits the activity.”

Before listening, learner states a purpose/question; during listening records or remembers key steps; afterwards paraphrases the message and asks a relevant clarification. A second reading deliberately replaces “return box” with “blue box” without providing a blue box; learner identifies the ambiguity and asks which box, rather than invent the answer. Do not supply the printed script during an independent listening check.

Teacher then says the original line “There is room for your idea” with a welcoming tone and an open-hand gesture, and repeats it with a puzzled questioning tone while pointing to a crowded model plan. Ask how context, tone and gesture change the possible message. These are declared enacted examples, not universal meanings of one face or gesture. Offer accessible spoken/visual description; no eye contact, posture or emotional-display norm is scored. Grades4–6 explain/compare why a gesture may be understood differently in different cultural or individual contexts, asking the speaker rather than diagnosing an identity.

## O2 — Formal and informal exchange

Original scripted teaching model.

Informal partner: “I cannot find the time. Could you show me?”
Reply: “The time is below the book picture. Would a larger line help?”
Formal report to a responsible adult: “Our model has two activities. We recommend separate times because the responses differ. The route still needs your approval. Could you explain which space must stay clear?”
Follow-up: “You said the route beside the wall is needed. Have I understood correctly?”

Learner role-plays a different clarification/elaboration and then takes part in an actual permitted small-group and larger-group discussion. Actual responses are not pre-written. Record asking relevant questions, paraphrasing/restating, responding to another contribution, purpose/audience, precise vocabulary/cohesion and a suitable next step. Grades5–6 justify strategy choices and analyze their effect; a rehearsed script alone is not independent spontaneous communication.

## L1–L6 — Original literary-device miniatures

L1: “Busy bees buzz by. / Tap, tap: rain is near. / A star is bright / In the dark of night.” Rhyme bright/night, initial /b/ alliteration, sound words buzz/tap; explain the effect, not only the label.
L2: “Pack a stack of thick books. / Our cart rolled like a little boat.” Consonance repeats /k/ across pack/stack/thick/books; the like-comparison suggests movement, not a literal boat. Contrast “I like books”, which reports preference and makes no simile.
L3: “Slow gold light crossed the road. / Our cart was a bridge between readers.” Slow/gold/road repeat a vowel in the intended ordinary pronunciation; accept a supported account in the learner's accent. The bridge is a metaphor for connection, not a crossing people can walk on.
L4: “The shelves whispered welcome.” Compare the original short narrative: “Fox put on his school backpack. ‘I hope Ms Owl likes my story,’ he said.” Explain personification versus an animal represented as a human-like character, and the effect of BOTH; do not infer actual fox behaviour.
L5: “Dry pages rustled like leaves; a thin stripe of warm light lay across the table.” Explain sensory imagery. “I labelled the label box LABELS, then made a label explaining my label.” Explain the recursive humour and its purpose/audience; not every reader must find it funny.
L6: “I had a million ideas, but I still had to break the ice.” Explain hyperbole and idiom in the N6 context; distinguish an exaggerated quantity from a counted total and social introduction from literal ice breaking. Ask for a fresh interpretation in a different supplied context.
