# Language source operations: words, fluency, sentences and conventions

Original executable teacher/assessor material for the source-operation supplement, not a new topic or tracked difficulty scheme. Grade references identify existing source duties. The actual pupil may use any appropriate source resource with honest support records; grade profiles do not create six versions of a claim. Demonstrate with the teaching examples, remove completed answers, and assess with the fresh examples. Keep original attempts and genuine corrections. No fixed speed, accent, handwriting grip or zero-error threshold is invented. Support that supplies the target operation is recorded as support, not independent success. The complete broader contributor criteria remain separate.

## F2-G1 — spoken sounds, letters, words and fluent meaning

**Sound-only B2.1.** Hide all print and pictures. Adult says the separate phonemes of “she”, “map”, “ship”, “frog” and “jump”, with brief natural pauses and no added “uh”; pupil blends each into the spoken word. Adult pronunciation key: she /ʃ/ /iː/; map /m/ /æ/ /p/; ship /ʃ/ /ɪ/ /p/; frog /f/ /r/ /vowel in the adult's ordinary frog/ /g/; jump /dʒ/ /ʌ/ /m/ /p/. Respect normal accent variants. Separately ask pupil to isolate initial/final and an appropriate medial sound, then segment whole spoken words into phonemes. A printed letter count is not this operation: “ship” has three phonemes in ordinary pronunciation; “box” has four, including /k/ /s/. Fresh unshown words: “me”, “sun”, “fish”, “stop”, “hand”. Record blending, isolation and segmentation separately. This preserves the actual spoken-blending claim and its existing different-number/cluster and contrast repertoire; it does not award printed decoding or all manipulation skills.

**Letters B2.2.** Supply alphabet cards A–Z and a–z and a blank writing surface. Name/form all uppercase and lowercase letters in order and in a fresh shuffled order. Reproducible shuffle: M a T q B f Y n D s H w E r K u C x O g J v L p I z. Then name/form their opposite-case partners. Check alphabetic order with a different subset and missing letters. Observe increasingly ready access across trials; one recited alphabet, copied line or prompt-induced response is not automaticity. Adult models the school's readable formation pattern; adapted access is recorded and not replaced by a speed/grip norm.

**Common correspondences B2.3.** Maintain a domain record across the common taught consonant/short-vowel correspondences and common combinations, not one correct word. Teaching bank: m,s,t,p,n,f,b,d,g,l,r,h,j,w,v,z; a/e/i/o/u short-vowel examples; c/k/ck, sh/ch/th/ng/qu, x; and the taught common vowel spellings ai/ay, ee/ea, oa/ow, igh, oo, ar/or/er as appropriate to the learner's actual pronunciation. Examples include map, sun, bed, sit, hot, cup, cat, kit, back, ship, chat, thin, this, sing, quit, box, rain, day, see, sea, boat, snow, light, moon, car, fork, her. “th” has different sounds in thin/this; x can represent /k/+/s/. Read the grapheme, give its sound in context, then spell a dictated word using it. Check correspondence retrieval in both directions across the domain; this illustrative bank is not a closed national word list or proof from a single sample.

**Phonics B2.4.** Pupil blends printed map/ship/stop, reads the sentences “Sam put a book in a bag” and “The ship is big”, then segments dictated regular words and writes them. Match text to taught correspondences; explicitly teach necessary familiar exception words rather than pretend every word is phonetically regular. Use unseen regular pin/fish/hand and a fresh short sentence for transfer. A picture or adult whole-word answer does not demonstrate decoding/spelling.

**Orthography B2.5.** Compare cat/kit/back: choose a plausible spelling for /k/ using taught positional patterns; explain why back ends in ck after a short vowel in this common pattern. Treat tendencies as tendencies, not universal rules. Fresh sack/duck and another known context; accept checked exceptions. The separate Alternative Spellings examples are fall with a before ll and bare/bear as homophones; walk has lk and quiet/quite are not a homophone pair. Do not infer a unique spelling from sound alone when context is required.

**Meaning/morphemes B2.6–.7.** Build help→helps/helpful, play→replay/played and kind→unkind with cards; read/spell and explain how each meaningful part changes the word. Explicitly teach label, return and revise from the corpus; use each in another context, including a science/art task. Fresh helpful/unhelpful or a new taught base requires analysis plus accurate reading/spelling, not a guessed picture. A base/prefix/suffix explanation does not certify every broader morphological criterion.

**Fluency B2.8.** Read familiar words, a short sentence and an appropriate N1 paragraph independently for meaning, then aloud with suitable expression/pacing. Discuss the message and correct a genuine miscue by looking back. A fresh F-story portion and another form check transfer. Record accuracy, phrasing, pacing and comprehension; neither one memorised line nor adult read-aloud establishes independent fluent reading. The corpus offers options; the teacher checks taught-word compatibility rather than scoring untaught vocabulary as failure.

## F2-G2 — consolidated decoding and developing word knowledge

B2.1: use phonemic blending/segmentation for regular mono- AND multisyllabic examples: sun, stop, sunset, picnic, jumping. Distinguish phonemes from syllables and letters. Demonstrate set for variability with an approximation of a familiar irregular spelling, such as bread read with the more usual ea vowel: recheck letters and context, try a known spoken alternative and verify meaning, rather than guess from an image. Teach before assessing; regular-word dictation remains regular. Fresh word set: lunch, hand, sandpit, resting, with taught correspondences checked.
B2.2: apply/check position-based c/k/ck and common vowel/ending tendencies in read-and-spell contexts; include a known exception, and use sentence meaning to choose bare/bear. A tendency is not a universal pronunciation law.
B2.3: read/spell replay, careless, helpful, jumping; identify base/prefix/suffix and meaningful change. Fresh reread, kindness and a teacher-verified familiar base/affix combination; do not infer that adding any affix creates a real word.
B2.4: explicitly teach response, timetable, route and revise from I1–I3; use in the story, another subject and an own explanation; analyze a new related form such as revised/revision with appropriate teaching of the spelling change.
B2.5: independently read words, sentences and paragraphs from appropriate N2, information and literary materials, then a fresh passage, with accuracy, meaning, phrasing/expression/intonation appropriate to purpose. Use the domain record and actual miscues/corrections, no WPM criterion or adult reading counted as pupil fluency.

## F2-G3 — consolidated phonics/orthography and growing morphology

B2.1: retain actual regular monosyllabic/multisyllabic read-and-dictate operations and set-for-variability correction from G2, using taught examples sunset, fantastic, jumping and a fresh word set. Explicitly distinguish syllable breaks from phoneme segmentation. The precise pronunciation belongs to the learner's normal English variety, not a forced accent.
B2.2: compare hop→hopping and hope→hoping with explicitly taught short-vowel doubling/final-e patterns; choose/read/spell a fresh checked pair such as rob→robbing and ride→riding. State what the pattern does and its scope; do not turn a useful tendency into every-word law.
B2.3: analyze/read/spell rereading, unhelpful, playful and a fresh related combination, identifying bases/prefixes/suffixes and meaning. Spelling transformations are taught/checked, not invented by attaching letters blindly.
B2.4: teach inference, evidence, sequence and habitat in the relevant corpus/science contexts; use in own sentences and explain a newly encountered related form with contextual and morphological evidence.
B2.5: read appropriate words, COMPLEX sentences and paragraphs across narrative/information/poem forms, adjusting oral expression/intonation to purpose. Explain the cause relationship in “Because the path was slippery, the club stayed inside.” Fresh N4/F material after the taught example tests transfer. Stronger source reading does not waive older complete contributor operations.

## F2-G4 — word meaning, morphological reading/spelling and fluency

B2.1: use base/affix meanings to read and spell unhelpful, rereading, careless, disagreement, then fresh unfairness/reusable after teaching relevant transformations. Explain how parts contribute meaning and check actual spelling; phonetic approximation alone is not the whole morphological operation.
B2.2: explicitly teach credibility, accessible, perspective and evidence with sentence contexts; contrast a claim with its evidence, use vocabulary in another subject and analyze related credible/incredible or access/accessible with actual spelling taught. No invented rule that every base takes able rather than ible.
B2.3: read a variety of appropriate N4/I/poem/actual creator texts independently, then aloud with expression, intonation, accuracy and purposeful pacing. Give a fresh appropriate portion and meaning question. Neither reading every genre in the same way nor a speed score replaces comprehension.

## F2-G5 — generalized morphological use

B2.1: read/spell reconstruction, disagreement, unpredictable and happiness with meaningful base/affix analysis and the taught spelling changes. Explain reconstruct/reconstruction and happy/happiness rather than merely sound out. Fresh reusable, misreading and a new appropriately taught derived form test more ready generalized use across contexts. Observe accuracy/readiness over different texts; no fixed latency criterion.
B2.2: teach bias, reliability, inference and proportion in context, use in the story/media/another subject and analyze related reliable/unreliable or interpret/interpretation with explicit teaching where spelling changes. A supplied definition alone is not productive vocabulary use.
B2.3: read narrative, information/persuasive and literary forms fluently with purpose-sensitive oral expression/intonation and comprehension. Revisit a genuine difficulty with a strategy and independently read another suitable text. Adult support and text familiarity remain recorded.

## F2-G6 — complex morphological vocabulary and flexible fluent reading

B2.1: analyze/read/spell misinterpretation, reconsideration, unhelpfulness and unpredictability; identify plausible meaningful parts, explain context and apply taught spelling changes. Fresh reconstructed, unreliability and a new checked derived word test generalized accuracy/readiness, not a recited affix list or a fabricated invented word.
B2.2: teach implicit, corroborate, accessible and interpretation through actual contexts, use precisely across reading/media/science or civic learning, and test an unfamiliar related form using meaning/morphology/reference checking. Prior familiarity with adult technical words is not assumed.
B2.3: read diverse appropriate texts accurately and with suitable pacing/phrasing, adjust oral expression/intonation to narrative, explanation or persuasive purpose, and explain meaning. A fresh complex F-extension and a chosen authentic text portion test transfer with honest support records, rather than a single rehearsed excerpt.

## F3-G1 — sentence forms, parts and end marks

Teaching inputs: “The book is red.” (declarative); “Put it here.” (imperative); “Can it fit?” (interrogative); “What a bright sign!” (exclamatory); “Sam reads, and Jo draws.” (compound with two independent clauses). Identify type/function and construct own examples, including a compound; a raised voice is not the definition of an exclamation.
Parts: book/books, box/boxes (common singular/plural nouns); “I read” (capital pronoun I); “We played / We play / We will play” (past/present/future); “a small cart” (adjective); “moves slowly” (adverb); “Sam read and Jo drew” (conjunction). Explain each function in meaning and use it in an own sentence, not just label an isolated word.
Edit input: “i put a book here can it fit” → a possible edit “I put a book here. Can it fit?” Explain sentence starts and end marks. Fresh child-facing input: “sam has a bag where is it” with own supported segmentation/capitals/end marks; teacher key “Sam has a bag. Where is it?” Alternative punctuation is accepted when it fits the expressed meaning.

## F3-G2 — grammar functions and direct speech

Identify/construct the four sentence functions and compound forms with new content. Explain courage (abstract noun), team (collective noun), she/they/I (personal pronouns), am/is/are/was/were (“to be” forms), tall/taller/tallest and good/better/best (comparison), and/but/because (conjunction roles). Show which word an adverb modifies: “Mira moved slowly”→moved; “a very bright sign”→bright; “rather slowly”→slowly. Use these functions in new communication, not a memorized word class list.
Edit input: “sam packed pens books and cards jos bag was full ‘i can help’ said mira” → one version “Sam packed pens, books, and cards. Jo's bag was full. ‘I can help,’ said Mira.” Explain proper-noun capitals, list commas, possessive apostrophe and direct-speech quotation marks. The serial comma is a consistent style choice, not a universal source requirement. A fresh short message supplies different names/list/possessor/quotation for independent correction.

## F3-G3 — clauses, possessives and dialogue

Identify/construct “The club opened” (simple), “The club opened, and visitors came” (compound), “Because rain began, the club moved inside” (complex with adverbial clause). Combine own related ideas and explain how the dependent clause adds time/reason/condition.
Functions: “Sam's book / the students' signs” (possessive nouns); “The sign is bright” (linking verb) versus “Mira is painting” (progressive verb phrase); “Which book is yours?” (interrogative adjective/determiner); “Where did it go?” (interrogative adverb); “under the table / between the boxes” (prepositions); “Oh!” (interjection). Explain meaning and use in an own sentence.
Edit input: “jo said, ‘we can't move it.’ our poem is called in our room” → “Jo said, ‘We can't move it.’ Our poem is called ‘In Our Room’.” Explain dialogue capitals, a consistent title style, quote/comma placement and contraction apostrophe. ‘In Our Room’ is the original L1 miniature for this task. Fresh dialogue/title/contraction input and own paragraph test use beyond copying. Distinguish it's from possessive its where taught; do not manufacture an error in the pupil's work.

## F3-G4 — relative/perfect/question/command forms and proper adjectives

Inputs: “The cart that Mira built has a large label” (relative clause with that); “The reader who asked a question found the time” (who); “Mira has moved the cart” (perfect); “Has Mira moved the cart?” (interrogative verb arrangement); “Move the cart” (imperative). Use pronoun-related forms adjectivally: “this sign / that cart / our books”; compare “This is clear”, where this stands alone. Modern descriptions may call the noun-modifying uses determiners; assess their FUNCTION rather than demand a false claim that every this/that is a relative pronoun.
Construct simple and compound sentences with a prepositional phrase and independent/dependent clauses as appropriate; explain the relationship in a fresh passage. Do not turn an optional advanced form into a new extra source gate.
Correct “We saw a canadian flag, japanese lettering and a victorian house” → Canadian/Japanese/Victorian with derivations Canada/Japan/Queen Victoria; common adjectives remain lowercase. Direct address: “Jo, please read” and “Please read, Jo.” Mark a short-work title in quotation marks: “We read ‘In Our Room’.” Fresh adjective/address/title cases require own use; lexicalised variable-capitalisation expressions are excluded from this specific task.

## F3-G5 — complete sentence variety and precise punctuation

Retain the actual original T9IX whole stimulus: “Rain hammered the roof. The players wanted to train. The field was flooded. The path was slippery. The coach moved everyone indoors. The storm eased. The team stayed inside. The coach planned a game.” Rewrite deliberately with SIMPLE, COMPOUND, COMPLEX and COMPOUND-COMPLEX forms and explain effects. Possible examples: “Rain hammered the roof”; “The players wanted to train, but the field was flooded”; “Because the path was slippery, the coach moved everyone indoors”; “Although the storm eased, the team stayed inside, and the coach planned a game.” Classify “The girl who won the race waved, and everyone cheered” as compound-complex: two independent clauses plus a dependent relative clause. One compound-complex answer does not award the complete four-form/style claim.
Fragments/run-ons: repair “Because the path was wet” with a suitable independent clause; repair “The club opened visitors came” by valid sentence division or conjunction. Explain why the chosen form communicates the relationship. Fresh uncompleted inputs: “Although the room was small”; “Mira drew Noah checked the plan”. Accept multiple grammatical, meaningful repairs.
Functions: “The coach praised the twins / Ava found her keys” show direct objects as people/things; “Mira herself wrote the note” is intensive emphasis; “Mira reminded herself to check” is reflexive; Canadian is a proper adjective; “both Mira and Noah / either a label or a timetable” use correlative conjunctions. Use in own sentences and explain function.
Punctuation inputs: “Mira, our club's designer, revised the plan” (appositive); “Carrying a folder, Mira entered” (initial participial phrase modifying Mira); “Bring these items: a book, a pen, and a card” (colon after complete imperative clause). Explain relationships; not every phrase takes commas. Fresh suitable nouns/phrases/list test transfer.
The broader hzkN eleven-term claim is preserved with these actual inputs: subject/object in the two direct-object sentences; active “The cat chased the mouse” versus passive “The mouse was chased by the cat”; big/large synonyms and big/small antonyms; “We opened the box and found … nothing” and the original full notice “Visitors must read the plan and sign in at the office” versus its shortened quotation “Visitors must … sign in at the office” for omitted-word ellipsis; “a well-known author” versus “The gym — finally dry — reopened on Monday” for sentence-setting dashes; the list-introducing colon above; “The rain stopped; we went outside” for a semi-colon linking independent clauses; and a vertical three-rule bullet list (supplied below). 

- Listen when others speak.
- Put tools away.
- Ask before borrowing.

Explain each function in context. All eleven terms remain required for that whole claim, while the narrower Ontario object duty alone awards none.

## F3-G6 — effective relative clauses, gerunds, voice and colons

Construct and explain “The cart that has large labels stands near the wall” and “Our cart, which has large labels, stands near the wall”, identifying how a relative/adjective clause relates ideas and whether it identifies or adds information about the cart. Use a fresh relationship in an own meaningful sentence; grammatical labels alone are insufficient.
“Reading helps Mira relax” uses Reading as a noun/gerund; “Mira is reading” uses reading in a verb phrase. Explain function rather than call every ing-form a noun. Convert “Jo printed the labels” ↔ “The labels were printed by Jo”; preserve tense/meaning and explain active/passive focus, including the agent omitted in “The labels were printed”. Select active/passive appropriately in a fresh oral report and text, without assuming passive always hides blame.
Actual formal models: “Dear Club Members:” in a specified formal-letter style; “Reading Club Members:” as a specified memo salutation; “Jo: Please check the time.” in script dialogue; “However, the route still needs approval” and “For example, a larger label may help” for transitional commas. Explain which function each colon/comma serves. Other legitimate styles are not universally wrong; demonstrate the requested source use rather than enforce one format on every text.
Fresh edit/recast input: “dear visitors however we have not checked the route jo said the labels were printed by mira” → one appropriate formal/script separation “Dear Visitors: / However, we have not checked the route. / Jo: The labels were printed by Mira.” Explain capitalization, salutation/speaker colons, transition comma and passive, then produce a different own version. Retain the actual word-processing/spelling/grammar-checker and cursive/keyboarding evidence from the separately qualified public task; automated suggestions are checked, not assumed correct.

## Fresh complex reading and grammar transfer

Unworked original narrative: “While Asha checked the timetable, Noah prepared a report. Asha found two sessions with the same starting time. She asked which group would use the room first; the notes did not say. Noah's report called the plan complete because every box had a label. When he heard Asha's question, he added a section called ‘What we still need to know’. A visitor then asked for an audio version. ‘We can read the timetable aloud today,’ Asha said, ‘but we have not yet made a recording.’ Noah crossed out ‘audio available’ and wrote the actual arrangement. The room was not magically perfect. The information had become more honest.”

Keys, withheld during independent work: two strands are checking times and preparing a report; they meet through Asha's clarification and the visitor request. Labels do not establish a complete timetable. Reading aloud now and a reusable recording are different modalities; no nonexistent recording is claimed. Asha's conditional spoken reply does not promise every future access arrangement. Ask the learner to summarize, infer a changed view, choose a supported title, rewrite another viewpoint and identify a needed source/permission. Then create/revise an own text and apply the grade's specific word/grammar operations with new content. An adult completed answer is never the learner's independent evidence.
