# Preparing paper for an actual drawing session

Original Grade1 C4 source-authoring resource. No canonical edits, new claim, learner-state change or current disposition is proposed here. This supplies the actual prediction/check/revision operation absent from the saved Connecting maths to real life guide; that guide retains its separate representation, unit, reasonableness and comparison duties. No actual session, observation or pupil performance has occurred.

## The outside-mathematics need

Choose a real forthcoming drawing activity in the child's classroom, home or club. Ask the actual organiser what paper people need and which usable sheets are available. Keep numbers within50 and simplify arithmetic when necessary without inventing the answers. Use generic place markers rather than recording people's names. An adult confirms that materials and arrangements suit the real activity. If no actual activity or organiser is available, this is preparation only; a fictional worksheet is not relabelled a real-world check.

Give the learner blank paper, pencil, counters/markers, a blank sheet headed 'what we need / what we know / our prediction / actual check / decision', and access to the actual paper stack and places to prepare. No finished model, supplied inventory or worked answer is given. Reading and writing support may record the learner's own decisions; the adult does not supply the mathematical relation or revision.

## An entire modelling cycle

1. **Understand the practical problem.** The learner explains the intended activity and what a successful paper preparation would achieve. Ask the organiser whether each participant needs one sheet, whether any person needs more, and whether a spare sheet is wanted. Establish what counts as a usable sheet for this activity. The learner identifies the quantities that matter and states a provisional assumption, rather than selecting operations from keywords.
2. **Create and analyse a model.** Count the expected places and represent the initial allocation using counters, an own drawing or number sentence. For example, only if the actual organiser requests one sheet at each of six places plus one spare, the learner's model might show six place markers and one spare, with a prediction of seven sheets. Six/seven are an explanation of structure, never a supplied actual attendance or outcome. Explain what each object, number and operation represents. State a prediction of paper needed, compare it with an independently counted usable stock, and decide whether enough is available. Choose an appropriate mathematical representation and explain why it helps.
3. **Check outside the model.** Before accepting the plan, lay out the predicted sheets at the actual prepared places and ask the organiser to check the provision against the actual activity. Count unused sheets or shortages and record what actually happened. Check that all relevant places and any requested extra uses were included. An arithmetic answer that is reasonable on paper is not this physical/organiser check. Do not manufacture a failure: an adequate model may survive this check, and that is a result to explain honestly.
4. **Assess and revise.** Explain whether the original assumptions matched the practical need and what limits remain, such as a late attendee or paper being unsuitable. If an actual discrepancy is found, change the allocation/model, predict the revised amount before acting, and check the revised arrangement again. If the first plan fits, retain it with a reason; then ask the actual organiser for a genuine feasible change to the session requirement, such as an additional prepared place or a request that each participant uses a second sheet. Label this explicitly as a new requirement, not an observed error. Revise the model and predict/check the new provision. If no real requirement changes, use a clearly stated conditional extension for reasoning, but do not call it an already performed real check.
5. **Communicate the decision.** The learner explains the final supply decision to the organiser, using quantities with their meaning (sheets, places, spare sheets), shows the model and actual record, and identifies why the plan was kept or revised. They distinguish the prediction, what was actually checked, and a hypothetical future condition.

## Independent source assessment

After a separate worked teaching example, prepare a fresh actual small drawing setup or a genuine changed supply requirement. Remove the worked model. The learner identifies the need and relevant quantities, chooses/constructs a representation, predicts the supply and shortage/surplus if relevant, carries out the actual check, and assesses/revises for the new requirement or actual discrepancy. Their own explanation must connect the mathematical answer to the practical decision. Correct calculation, a supplied bar model, naming objects, or simply repeating a unit does not establish the whole source process. Adult completion of the model or check is recorded as support, not independent performance.

Keep the completed source record and any whole-topic assessment separate. Grade1 C4 is independently assessed, not inferred from B/C/E labels, a problem-solving worksheet or a collection of linked topics. The actual numbers and outcomes remain blank until the real activity is done.
