# Coding and emerging technology: consequences and decisions

These are original, untested teaching and assessment materials. Product and trade facts are attributed below; practice observations and errors are fictional. A pupil does not need a phone, account, workshop or private recording. The cases assess consequences and judgments, not product operation or trade competence.

## Programmed reporting changes what people receive

Use the grade's existing [shadow investigation recipe](shadow-observation-recipes.md). In its invented practice table, the near, middle and far observations are 18, 12 and 9 cm. In the original changed case, a program incorrectly pairs the near label with 9 cm. These are fictional bookkeeping examples, not new physical predictions. Ask which information a partner receives, how the wrong pairing could change their comparison, and what must be checked before sharing the report. Then use the corrected pairing and an unmeasured trial. An honest “not recorded” message differs from presenting zero as a measurement.

A suitable explanation links the programmed instruction, its output and the recipient's decision: a wrong lookup can give a partner misleading information; checking the table-to-label relationship and retesting can correct this case. Saying “computers help us” or naming a tablet does not explain an impact. Do not claim all software is accurate after one test, that the pupil measured these figures, or that the picture model predicts every lighting arrangement. At delivery, the overall task uses the pupil's actual saved program and run records as well as this supplied comparison.

## AI captioning can help, with limits

Google's December 2024 account describes Expressive Captions as AI models producing stylised text and sound labels from audio. Its examples include emphasis and background sounds. The current Android help page describes automatic speech captions, device/language limits and clearer performance with clear speech and little background noise. This supplies evidence for an AI application; an automatic device alone is not proof of AI.

Original fictional case: Lee wants to follow a short science explanation without relying on hearing its audio. A provided transcript says “do not move the screen”; an invented draft caption omits “not”. This is an authored error for reasoning, **not an observed Google result or error-rate claim**. Ask how captions could make information more accessible, what this particular omission would change, and what Lee or a partner could check. A checked transcript or clarification can help; guessing from an uncertain caption is not equivalent to checking it. No learner must disclose hearing ability or perform an imitation of disability. Pointing to a picture or hearing a read-aloud version can elicit the same consequence reasoning without making reading fluency the sole test.

Change the case: the device/language combination is unsupported or the audio is unclear. What information or another accessible format is needed? AI output is useful in some conditions, not guaranteed truth. This case is one component of the existing AI topic; it does not assess that topic's entire example inventory. Do not claim every caption is AI generated or that every service processes data privately in the same way. No classroom audio is collected or uploaded.

## Skilled trades: a machinist checks a program

Skilled Trades Ontario describes general machinists using conventional and numerically controlled equipment and checking dimensions. Its published 2016 training logbook, sections 5239.03 and 5239.09–10, includes entering program data, verifying and inspecting the result, and retaining the verified program. These are professional duties, not primary-school operating instructions.

Original fictional comparison: a drawing calls for a part with two holes. A supplied screen-only representation of a machine program requests three. A trained machinist finds the mismatch before approving production. Ask what repeating the uncorrected instructions could do to parts, how checking changes that outcome, and why a useful stored program still needs correct setup and inspection. Reuse can support repeat work; a mistake can also be repeated. These are reasoned consequences of this supplied case, not measured productivity, cost or employment outcomes. Change the drawing to require three holes: the old “correction” must be checked against the new purpose rather than memorised.

The comparison links coding to a genuine skilled trade and to measurement/design in STEM. CNC is not automatically AI. A simple classroom drawing is not a calibrated machine simulation. No pupil operates equipment, writes executable CNC commands, performs a dry run/test cut, uses workshop tools or receives a competence certificate.

## Grade 1: impact assessment

Give the two everyday cases as short read-aloud/picture accounts. Ask the learner to identify who uses the report/captions, describe how changed instructions or captions affect the information they receive, and explain a benefit and a possible problem. Change one label or omit “not” and ask what difference it makes. “Tablet”, “robot” or “it is clever” alone is insufficient. For the A2 overall, the learner also executes the Grade 1 precise science sequence and uses its actual run to explain the reporting consequence.

## Grade 2: impact assessment

Use the reporting and caption cases. Have the learner connect a particular program part/output to what a partner learns, identify the caption application's access benefit and possible misleading output, and explain checking after the changed case. For the A2 overall, connect this reasoning to the actual decomposed-and-rejoined Grade 2 science program; planning alone does not replace execution.

## Grade 3: impact assessment

Ask the learner to trace the wrong label to the report consequence, propose a check, and explain why the caption example can both help and mislead. Change the data to unrecorded rather than zero and change the caption's missing word. For the A2 overall, use the actual Grade 3 test/refinement record to justify the correction and what remains uncertain; a single repaired example does not establish universal accuracy.

## Grade 4: impact assessment

Use all three cases, including the machinist. Ask the learner to relate the reporting output to an audience, the caption system to accessible communication, and the checked machine program to part quality. Explain a benefit, a limit and a checking decision for each relevant system. For the A2 overall, show the actual Grade 4 text/graphic outputs and explain their different purposes before comparing the STEM/trade case. An attractive screen or a named occupation is insufficient.

## Grade 5: impact assessment

Use all three cases. Compare the effect of storing a wrong observation with retaining a corrected series, explain caption uncertainty and appropriate verification, and explain how a verified trade program supports repeat work while incorrect instructions can repeat defects. For the A2 overall, include the actual Grade 5 storage/processing run, reset and changed-data check. Assess the effects on users and work; do not invent output rates, savings or guaranteed safety.

## Grade 6: impact assessment

Use all three cases. Explain the chain from input and programmed/model behaviour to information or part output, then the consequence for its user. Compare alternatives when the input is unknown, a caption is uncertain or a design has changed. Justify an appropriate check or accessible alternative, including the machinist's role in STEM/trades. For the A2 overall, execute the Grade 6 communication-mode versus scientific-trial inputs for their distinct purposes and use the actual run as evidence. More controls or more AI alone do not establish a better outcome.

## Evidence and limits

Across grades, assess the actual source demand through the relevant case explanation. For an overall, require both actual science coding and a reasoned impact judgment in one portfolio; two linked child codes are not an assessment. Each changed case must preserve the age-appropriate source duty. Missing access support, unavailable equipment, private non-disclosure or an unread text alone is not evidence of a conceptual gap.

Prepared tasks do not certify any child's performance. Existing whole claims, dependencies and age scopes remain unchanged. The five-example AI repertoire and broad historical technology-change topic remain separate. No numerical social benefit, universal AI privacy rule or classroom efficacy is asserted.
