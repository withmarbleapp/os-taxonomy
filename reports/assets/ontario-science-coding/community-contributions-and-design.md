# Practical design and scientific contributions

Original, untested task materials for six grade portfolios. These assess practical reasoning with supplied evidence, not a memorised national or cultural repertoire. Use accessible read-aloud, drawing, pointing, discussion and writing while retaining the learner's own explanation. Neither a list of subjects nor a list of famous names demonstrates the source duty.

## Practical problem: shade and a useful explanation

For Grade 1, establish a useful classroom problem: compare small cover designs for keeping a model play area from warming as much in sunlight. Use the existing shade-building task, with its actual design, construction, matched shaded/unshaded comparison and one improvement. Let the learner choose coverage and record what actually happens over equal times. The science explains sunlight/warming; mathematics compares coverage and the observed temperatures using accessible relative language; the class's shared report explains which design the evidence supports and its limits. Have a partner try to understand the report, then revise it if necessary. Ask what would be missing if the learner only decorated a roof, only counted materials, or wrote a recommendation without testing it.

The purpose is reduced warming, not a claim that a toy model is a certified shelter. Use small stable structures, adult-prepared materials and suitable instruments. An adult checks local weather, exposure, ground conditions and equipment; pupils never look towards the Sun, look through a cover at it, touch potentially hot surfaces or use a hot lamp. Do not infer temperature from darkness alone. Unexpected clouds, a moved shadow or inconclusive readings remain actual observations, not failed pupils. At delivery the physical comparison is required; a screenshot or invented “cooler” result cannot replace it.

Changed case: the target area moves beyond the cover or the available material changes. Explain which scientific observation, comparison and message must change. The task preserves the existing full shade claim and its underlying warming readiness; no new younger or climate-specific topic is introduced.

## Practical problem: deliver a soft object to a target

For Grades 2–6, a class needs a low, safe route for a large soft ball to reach a broad target tray. Agree the user and observable success goal before making a short cardboard ramp/guide on a clear floor. Use the same ball and release procedure, record several actual stop positions or target hits, then change one design feature and retest. Use no launcher, projectile, small choking-size marble, elevated route or pupil-carrying structure. The pupil uses the result, not enjoyment or the appearance of the model, to judge whether the design meets its purpose.

Science connects push/direction or ramp change to the motion observed. Mathematics makes comparisons usable: picture/token counts and ordered stop marks can work before numerical measurement; later learners can use consistently measured distances and a clear table. Language gives a partner an intelligible explanation, recommendation and limitation based on the record. These three contributions must actually interact: the comparison informs a design choice and the report tells the intended user what the evidence supports. A graph about an unrelated subject or three subject labels does not satisfy this task.

An original practice record may be used to rehearse analysis: Design A reaches the tray on two of four trials, Design B on three of four. These are invented figures, not an observation, a claim that B will always work or a statistical significance test. For assessment retain the learner's actual trial record, interpret discrepancies and decide what further testing is warranted. Change the target distance or route; use the evidence to reconsider the recommendation, without pretending the old trials tested the new situation. No mean, regression, equation or advanced force vocabulary is a universal gate.

## Evidence cases: communities contribute in different ways

Use the actual attributed cases and source/date/limit records in the [community conservation dossier](../../ontario-grade5-community-conservation-design-2026-11-11.json). Its three cases remain distinct: T’Sou-ke community planning and energy initiatives; Métis Nation of Ontario knowledge, harvest policy and monitoring roles; and the Nunatsiavut Imappivut knowledge-gathering and marine-planning account reported by ITK in 2019. These sources are publicly available accounts, not permission to extract private teachings, stage harvesting, claim an identity or speak for a community.

For each case, have the learner identify **who contributed, what knowledge or work they contributed, the scientific/technical decision it informed, and what difference that can make**. Separate a documented contribution to planning/monitoring from a measured ecological outcome. Renewable generation differs from reducing demand; a conservation principle or a harvest count alone does not prove species recovery; a possible protected area in the dated account is not an established one. Do not generalise one Nation or organisation's account to all First Nations, Métis or Inuit people.

Add this original short, attributed case: Katherine Johnson was a Black American mathematician working with NACA/NASA teams. NASA's biography describes her calculations for Alan Shepard's 1961 flight and her independent check of computer calculations for John Glenn's 1962 mission. The work helped teams plan and check flight paths. Her career also involved unequal access and segregated institutions. She was one contributor among many; neither her identity nor one check by itself explains a whole mission's success.

Ask the learner to compare contributions: community-held observations/values can inform resource decisions; mathematical analysis and checking can inform a flight plan. All involve people doing consequential knowledge work, with different methods, purposes and social contexts. Name recognition, praise, identity categories or a generic statement that “everyone helps” is insufficient. No orbital calculation, biography recall inventory, private community knowledge or first-person testimony is required.

Changed evidence brief: an imaginary poster shows a solar panel and says “this proves the community used traditional knowledge and all homes now use less energy.” The picture alone supports neither claim. Identify the missing process/outcome evidence and revise the conclusion. A second imaginary brief says “the computer checked itself, so human mathematical work made no contribution”; contrast that claim with the attributed Johnson case. Neither brief is an actual community statement or actual NASA finding.

## Shared inquiry and writing

Use the sources openly in a small shared project. A learner contributes an evidence-backed idea, draws or writes a fact and helps gather relevant information for the group's answer. Label an attributed source account separately from the class's model observation and its own inference. A teacher can read the material and record dictated words; the learner still makes the comparison and explains the contribution. This uses the existing Shared Research Projects operation as a component, not a new cultural-knowledge score or automatic whole-topic mastery from reading a case.

Produce a class comparison display or short report in which each learner's contribution remains observable. Ask a partner to explain what they learned from it, correct a misleading arrow or unsupported outcome claim, and revise the message. Sources remain available. The learner need not supply personal medical, family, community or identity information.

## Practical applications beyond the class model

For Grades 1–3, elicit two familiar applications: one in a home/residential setting and one in a school/community setting. Original picture choices can include a cover protecting an outdoor entrance/play space and a ramp helping move an object. Let the learner choose a setting they recognise without an address, household inspection or assumption that everyone has the same home. Explain the useful problem and the relevant warming or motion idea. A model made in class is not proof that a real building's cover or ramp has been tested. For the overall, relate this account to the actual class design and contribution comparisons.

For Grades 4–6, use both the actual [machinist program-checking case](everyday-impact-cases.md#skilled-trades-a-machinist-checks-a-program) and Johnson's mathematical work. Explain how material shaping, program verification and dimensional checking help the machinist make a part fit its design, and how calculation/checking helps a flight-planning team. Name the different occupations, the useful problem, the scientific/technical or mathematical work and why the result matters. Include the skilled trade explicitly; “engineers use science” alone is insufficient. No pupil operates machinery, solves orbital equations or receives occupational certification. The overall independently combines these practical occupational explanations with the class problem and plural contribution analysis.

These supporting application tasks revisit the six A3.1 source positions whose older mappings were labelled Full. Their historical label alone is not fresh task evidence. The later grade rationales' generic product/material examples do not, by themselves, establish the occupational/skilled-trade duty; the concrete cases above supply it without changing a claim or adding an occupation-specific topic.

## Grade 1: practical contributions portfolio

Complete the actual shade problem with a simple comparison and shared explanation. Use short read-aloud/picture accounts from at least two different community cases, plus the Johnson comparison with support. For each, explain what the people did and how it helped a real scientific/technical purpose; point to the account that supports the explanation. Distinguish an action from a wished-for result. The overall also asks why the shade has a useful purpose and how science, a comparison and communication helped choose it, retaining both familiar home/residential and school/community applications.

## Grade 2: practical contributions portfolio

Complete the soft-ball route, actual trial record, purposeful revision and shared report. Explain how the motion observation and mathematical comparison together support the chosen change. Compare at least two community cases and Johnson's team contribution, retaining people/work/purpose/consequence and a simple evidence limit. Apply the changed target and unsupported-poster cases. The overall joins practical application with actual contribution analysis and the two familiar-setting applications; it is not inferred from linked child labels.

## Grade 3: practical contributions portfolio

Use the real route tests and report to explain what each subject contributed to solving the stated problem. Distinguish an actual record from the invented practice table. Compare the community and Johnson accounts, identify a supported contribution and an unsupported outcome claim, and revise the message after a changed target or new source fact. Retain plural community contributions, people's different work and circumstances and both familiar-setting applications in the overall.

## Grade 4: practical contributions portfolio

Use consistent comparisons from real route trials to justify a design recommendation for its user; explain the roles of science, mathematics and a clear written/visual message. Compare all three community cases and Johnson's contribution, with the dated source accounts available. Explain what each contribution informed and what outcomes remain unproven. Complete the changed-target and unsupported-evidence briefs. The overall requires both the useful application and the diverse contribution comparison, including the two actual occupations and explicit skilled trade.

## Grade 5: practical contributions portfolio

Use the actual route portfolio to explain a scientific observation, a mathematical comparison and a communication decision that together address the user's need. Compare all three attributed community cases and the Johnson case for the knowledge/work, method, purpose and effect on decisions. Separate stated plans, performed work and measured outcomes. Connect energy/resource reasoning to the existing Grade 5 dossier where relevant; this does not turn its case names into a memorisation topic. The overall independently elicits the combined practical and contribution explanation after changed facts, including the two occupational cases and explicit skilled trade.

## Grade 6: practical contributions portfolio

Evaluate the route's actual evidence, repeatability and stated limitations, and explain why the design recommendation changes when the target/user need changes. Make the scientific, mathematical and language contributions explicit through the actual artifact. Compare all three community cases with Johnson's mathematical verification for their methods, lived/social contexts and decision effects. Challenge both unsupported briefs using evidence. The overall requires a reasoned practical application and plural contribution analysis, including both actual occupations and the explicit skilled trade; a successful device alone or a set of biographies is insufficient.
