# Grade 1: questions, evidence and useful designs

This original, untested programme provides repeated investigation opportunities across the four content strands. It is a prepared authoring design, not a record of children conducting the activities. Adults adapt entry points and support to readiness; the stages may be revisited rather than treated as a single compulsory linear script. No result is pre-filled or graded against a promised outcome.

## Prepare the investigation space

A responsible adult follows current school/board procedures, checks materials, allergies and individual access needs, selects appropriate protective clothing/equipment, demonstrates safe handling and makes an emergency/cleanup plan. Use small clean-water quantities, stable plastic trays and suitable plastic-cased measuring tools. Use only adult-approved untreated seeds and safe specimens; no tasting, mouth pipetting, unknown substances, heating, wildlife handling or deliberate environmental damage. An adult handles drilling, sharp preparation and mould disposal. Children use appropriate round-ended scissors and joining materials under supervision, carry/store tools safely, clear spills and wash hands. If the assessment requires protective clothing/equipment for the selected task, actually wear it; naming it is insufficient. Do not introduce a hazard just to create a PPE demonstration.

A hand lens is used indoors on the specimen, never directed towards the Sun or used to concentrate sunlight. For outdoor temperature work an adult checks weather, exposure and safe access. Do not look towards the Sun, look through a cover at it or touch a possibly hot surface. No hot lamp replaces the actual sunlight comparison.

Prepare three original blank records, on paper or accessible equivalents:

- **Question/source record:** question; how we will find out; source/observation and its author/date; what it tells us; what it cannot establish.
- **Trial record:** object/material/design; what changed; what stayed the same; prediction; actual observation or measurement; trial/date; question answered or still uncertain.
- **Design record:** intended user/need; success checks; available materials/time; sketch and reason; first test; feedback; change; retest; recommendation and limit.

A pupil can dictate to an adult, draw, point and explain, or write. The child's decisions and explanations must remain identifiable. A teacher's demonstration, copied conclusion or attractive worksheet alone is not the child's research, experimentation or design performance.

## Life systems: seeds and water

**Question and resources.** Offer untreated same-kind seeds and invite the child's questions. An original starting question is: “Over our observation period, what changes do we see when these seeds have a suitable amount of water, compared with seeds kept dry?” Read the selected Natural History Museum seed-growth account and the RHS investigation instructions listed below. Record who produced each account and which question it helps answer. An adult explains that seed starting and the later needs of a growing leafy plant are related but not identical; this comparison does not test every need of every living thing.

**Actual test.** Prepare matched labelled containers and the same kind of seed, safe medium and ambient conditions. The adult supports a comparison with water availability as the chosen change; do not also change seed type, location and temperature. Keep the moist condition suitable rather than waterlogged. Keep a dry comparison only for the agreed seed-starting period, not a starving experiment on an established plant. Observe at a consistent daily time over an adult-planned period suitable for the chosen seed; use more than one seed where practical. Draw or record the actual changes, including no visible change, and keep an honest dated record. Do not promise a sprout by a particular day. An adult checks for mould and maintains/transplants suitable seedlings afterwards; nothing is eaten.

Use the hand lens to examine two approved seeds or two safe leaves indoors. Describe actual visible colour, shape, texture and pattern, identify a similarity and a difference, and show what the lens reveals beyond the unaided view. Do not infer an unseen feature from a familiar label.

**Research and interpretation.** Combine the actual record with the named source account in a small shared written/drawn report. Distinguish firsthand observation, the publisher's account and the class's inference. If a seed does not sprout, do not infer that every dry seed is dead or that the named kind can never grow; consider conditions, viability and observation time. Explain what our comparison supports, what remains uncertain, one prediction for a changed case and one relevant improvement.

## Matter and energy: energy and a moving toy

**Question.** What changes when we supply a gentle push to the same large soft wheeled toy, compared with leaving it at the start without a push? Use a clear, level low route, marked start and adult-approved large toy; no projectile, riding, high drop or strong launch. Ask the pupil what makes the movement happen and what evidence would answer the question. The selected Ontario energy account concerns energy being needed for change; this activity is a bounded observation of supplied action and movement, not a measurement of energy or a complete force explanation.

**Actual comparison.** Observe the toy left at rest on the level route, then place it at the same start and give the agreed gentle push, recording what actually happens and where it stops. Repeat with the same toy, surface, start and gentle method, using marks or an appropriate simple distance record. Variation is retained, not replaced with an expected number. A push can start movement; do not teach that a continuing push is stored inside the toy or needed for every moment of subsequent movement. The comparison does not directly measure energy, isolate every frictional effect or prove that all energy is visible as motion.

Explain the observation using the idea that an input can produce a change, and connect it with another actually observed energy example from the sunlight task. Predict what might happen with a slightly different gentle push, test safely, compare prediction with actual result and suggest a relevant method improvement. A changed heavier toy needs a new comparison, not an unsupported numerical claim. Report the actual evidence and its limit in words, drawings and a simple headed record.

## Structures and mechanisms: two model covers

### Material-property investigation supporting the cover design

**Question.** Which of the selected materials keeps a small model paper area dry under our specified small water-drop test? Use equal-area plain paper, fabric and plastic samples approved by the adult. Observe two surfaces with the hand lens before testing; describe what is visible rather than guessing their performance from appearance. Science World's selected water-on-paper account supplies another source, not the answer to every material comparison.

**Actual test.** With the sample supported in the same simple frame over a dry indicator sheet in a tray, apply five similar small drops of clean water from the same safe dropper height. Change only the sample material. After the same agreed wait, record whether the back/indicator is wet, dry or unclear. Use fresh matched dry samples for repeated trials where practicable. Stop and revise a method that spills outside the intended area; a spill is not evidence that water passed through the material.

Record material, trial, prediction, actual observation and any uncertainty. For the question about repeated wet-through outcomes, a table and a simple pictogram of wet-through trial counts are meaningful: one symbol represents one trial, with headings, a consistent key and zero shown honestly. Do not make a decorative graph of unrelated categories or substitute an invented result. Explain why the representation helps compare the tested materials. Use a simple observable-feature sorting rule for the actual samples, show a labelled diagram and test whether a partner can follow it; the grouping is not “nice” versus “bad”.

Explain the supported conclusion, a changed-material prediction and a method improvement. The test concerns this water exposure and these samples, not strength, all waterproof products or performance in a real storm. The source's straw-blowing and colour extensions are not adopted.

**Useful design brief.** A partner wants a small model paper object to stay dry during the agreed five-drop test. It must stand over the object without someone holding it, using the supplied card/fabric/plastic, tape and safe tools within the session. Ask the intended user what matters and agree two observable checks: standing in place and keeping the target dry. The child explains how the shape/support and material choice are meant to meet the need; drawing alone is not the finished performance.

Sketch two possible covers, identify available-material/time constraints and choose how to make them. Cut, fold or roll card and a safe fabric piece where appropriate, choose and use a suitable joining method, and explain the shape-to-function relationship. The adult prepares any unsafe cutting or holes. Build and test both designs using the same target and water procedure, keep actual records and ask the partner whether the cover serves the agreed purpose.

Compare strengths and weaknesses using the test, not decoration or enjoyment. Change one relevant feature in response to a failure, feedback or uncertainty, then retest and revise the recommendation. If both leak or neither stands, that is useful evidence for another iteration. Changed case: the target becomes wider, or the available material changes. Explain why the previous tests do not automatically prove the revised design will work. These are small educational models, not building-weatherproofing certificates.

## Earth and space: actual sunlight and shade

**Different question.** A cover that keeps water off is not thereby known to keep an area cooler. A class wants a small model play area to warm less in sunlight. Use the existing full shade-building task and its corrected ground-shadow safety instruction. The child identifies the intended use, sketches/builds a small stable cover, selects a way to compare it with an uncovered area and explains why sunlight/warming is relevant.

Place comparable safe surfaces/instruments in shaded and unshaded positions for equal times, with an adult checking that the shade actually covers its target and that other conditions are as comparable as practical. Read and record the actual temperatures using suitable instruments; relative language can support interpretation, but darkness alone is not a temperature reading. Clouds, changed shade position or inconclusive readings are recorded, not replaced with a fabricated “cooler” value. Do not expose people or animals as test subjects or touch hot ground. Schedule a real safe sunlight opportunity; a picture, hot lamp or made-up data is not a substitute for this particular full claim.

Compare two small cover designs where feasible, explain which evidence supports a recommendation and improve/retest a feature. Changed case: the model target moves or the sun/shadow position changes. Predict what might change, identify what needs checking and keep an untested prediction distinct from an observation. The ground-shadow comparison concerns the cover; children never view the Sun through a material.

## Research, questioning and communication across the programme

Each learner generates at least three genuine questions across the investigations and chooses an appropriate route: firsthand observation, a controlled comparison, a relevant source or an informed adult. Distinguish an evidence question from a preference such as “Which cover is prettiest?” Do not require every question to become an experiment or rank books as inferior to observations. Adult support can sharpen a question without replacing the child's decision.

For shared research, the learner contributes a source-backed idea, draws/writes a fact and helps gather information for the joint answer. Source cards remain available. Ask who made an account, what it is for and whether it addresses our particular question; do not impose an advanced bibliography or universal formal bias-analysis inventory in Grade 1. Our actual result can differ from a general account because the material, conditions or question differ. Review missing/conflicting information honestly rather than silently change observations to fit the source.

Make an oral explanation and a short drawn/written report of actual findings, using relevant words such as seed, material, absorb, support, shade, temperature, observation, prediction and evidence when they fit. Choose a form for the intended purpose: a partner needs a labelled design/how-to explanation; a class comparison needs the actual record and conclusion. Use plan/draft/revise/edit language accurately where it applies; do not confuse revising a conclusion to match evidence with merely correcting spelling.

Ask a partner to explain what the report recommends and why. Revise a misleading label, unsupported conclusion or unreadable comparison in response. Retain the answer to the question, specific evidence, a new-case prediction, one relevant method improvement and the limit of the claim. Copying the teacher's conclusion, only naming science vocabulary or presenting a recount of steps is insufficient. The teacher retains ordinary classroom evidence at delivery; this public programme contains none.

## Sources and claim boundaries

- [Natural History Museum, Watching and learning how plants grow](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/take-part/activities-for-all/plant-club.html): selected actual seed-starting and observation text read. The source's “make energy” wording is not adopted; plants use light energy to make food. No image or video was inspected or copied.
- [RHS Schools, Store cupboard scientist](https://www.rhs.org.uk/education-learning/school-gardening/resources/curriculum-linked/store-cupboard-scientist): complete main investigation instructions read. Its edible-shoot suggestions are not adopted; seed treatment, allergy checks and no-tasting rules remain adult responsibilities. The original programme above does not import every suggested variable into one test.
- [Science World, Water Drop Races](https://www.scienceworld.ca/resource/water-drop-races/): complete main activity text read, no picture inspection. Its selected ordinary/waxed-paper comparison informs the resource discussion; our original cover/material protocol is not claimed to be the museum's tested intervention. Molecular attraction language, straw-blowing and colour extensions are not mastery demands.
- The actual Ontario 2022 scientific/engineering-process guidance allows supported, revisited stages. The programme supplies repeated opportunities across B/C/D/E, not evidence that every listed source component must be independently completed in every Grade 1 activity.
- Existing whole questions, close observation, simple-test, data, conclusion, model/design, safety and vocabulary claims remain unchanged. Component source coverage is not an automatic score for every full contributor or every hard prerequisite. In particular, chart/key readiness and broader vocabulary inventories stay explicit; the public tasks do not infer mastery transfer.
