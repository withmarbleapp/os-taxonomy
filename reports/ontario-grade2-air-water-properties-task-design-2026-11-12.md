## Materials and facts before assessment

Provide two different-shaped transparent plastic vessels, a spill tray, a small equal-volume water measure, a few ice pieces, safe rock and sand samples, a sealed flexible air bag, paper streamers, hand fan, and a clear plastic cup with a tissue held securely inside its base. Use a shallow water tray with adult supervision, no glass, hot kettle, child boiling, pressurised container, inhalation experiment or breath-holding task. The learner can direct an accessible assistant while making the prediction and explanation independently. No safety action is inferred as having happened now.

Teacher facts: ordinary air is a mixture of gases and generally invisible. It occupies space, has mass, can move and press against objects. A gas spreads through the available container and can be compressed; no formal pressure formula, atmospheric layers or particle-diagram gate belongs to this source task. Liquid water flows, occupies space and takes the lower part's shape; pouring the same amount between vessels does not create more water. Water has mass, and some materials dissolve in it while others do not. Use clean teaching materials but do not infer drinking safety from clarity, colour or smell. Air or water can carry contamination that cannot be detected by looking. No taste test, treatment recipe or health diagnosis is part of this portfolio.

These are a bounded age-appropriate property repertoire and observable comparisons, not a claim that the source printed an exhaustive property checklist. The existing state vocabulary claim also covers solids and gases beyond water; its independent whole assessment remains below.

## Teach and investigate: air is present and can act

Begin with a genuinely air-filled bag that looks empty of solid objects. Ask what is inside and how the learner could tell. Gently compress it with the opening closed, then open it and use its moving air to affect a paper streamer. Keep the bag away from faces; an adult-selected reusable demonstrator can replace it. Distinguish the air itself from the visible bag or moving paper. A still bag contains air too. The moving air makes the paper move; a tree does not make outdoor wind by waving.

Place the secured tissue inside the cup, predict what will happen, and slowly lower the inverted cup vertically into the shallow tray. Inspect the actual result. Then tilt it to release bubbles and observe water entering the space the air leaves. Use the result actually obtained: a leak, loose tissue or tilt during the first attempt can change it and requires a repeated controlled attempt, not a fictional dry-tissue record. The comparison supports air occupying space; it does not by itself measure air's mass. Supply the separately verified fact that air has mass. Compare two labelled models of otherwise identical rigid dry containers, one with more air and one with less, at the same conditions: the added air adds mass. No weighing success or quantitative density calculation is invented.

Use a hand fan gently, then more strongly, to compare effects on the same streamer. Use source-labelled drawings made by the teacher of a lightly fluttering flag, swaying branches and a severe gale bending a tree; state that these are models, not today's observed weather. Learner orders the effects and distinguishes breeze from gale. No exposure to severe weather is required. A warm breeze is still wind; strength and temperature are distinct. The source operation concerns moving air and effects, not the older cause-of-wind explanation.

## Teach and investigate: water's properties and changes

Learner pours one measured amount between a wide shallow and a tall narrow vessel, observes the changed shape, then returns it to the original measure. Record spills if any. A taller level alone does not show more water. Compare a solid rock, liquid water and contained air by their behaviour. Add actual sand grains as a near miss: a pile can pour while each grain remains solid. Use ordinary words and the state names together. A flexible container's shape alone does not prove its contents are liquid.

Observe a little ice melting in a tray and a teacher-prepared before/after freezing record with its actual date and conditions. The same water changes state; it is not a new material. For dissolving, compare a little teacher-selected food-grade salt stirred into a separate water sample with sand stirred into another. Do not consume the samples. Dissolved material has not ceased to exist, and not every solid dissolves. Melting does not mean mixing a second substance into a solvent. This comparison is a supplied practical property case, not an award of every chemical/materials investigation.

For the complete Heating & Cooling contributor separately retain actual observation and thermometer use with an ice-and-water mixture, recording the observed melting interval/reading; do not promise a perfect plateau with unsuitable apparatus. Provide a reliable normal-pressure reference showing pure water's0°C melting/freezing and100°C boiling values; pupils do not boil water or handle very hot/very cold materials. Distinguish a measured classroom result from a reference value. Draw two original schematic cards, each with12dots: a regular joined arrangement for solid water and an irregular moving arrangement for liquid water. Keep12dots in each and label the drawings as schematic, not to-scale measured particle positions. Add a separate pair of within-liquid warming cards with longer motion arrows on the warmer one. Use these to contrast warming within a state with a constant-temperature melting change: energy may increase motion within a state, while melting can change arrangement/interactions without requiring faster motion at the plateau. Retain all complete saved-guide checks, including another material's different change temperature/range and reversibility, as whole-topic extras, not a newly invented Grade2 source gate.

## E2.1 independent property assessment and changed case

After teaching, provide a fresh differently shaped air-containing vessel and a fresh pair of water containers. Ask the learner to choose and perform a comparison, explain what demonstrates air taking space and water changing shape without necessarily changing amount, and contrast still air with moving air. They must explain a changed sealed/open case, not repeat a supplied label. Ask how the evidence changes if the cup tilts or water spills; a correct answer must acknowledge the actual changed condition.

Present a new photograph/model of ice, liquid water and invisible water vapour represented by clearly labelled arrows, plus an actual dry rock, sand and air bag. Learner independently names states and justifies by behaviour, uses change-of-state wording, and distinguishes visible mist/cloud droplets from invisible vapour. The teacher's drawing of vapour is a representation; no one claims to see the gas itself. Ask whether clear water is therefore proven drinkable: no, the property observation is insufficient. Ask whether a faint breeze contains less real air than a still room: motion is not the test of existence. For mass, use a fresh otherwise identical-container comparison with different air contents and require the reason rather than recall of a numeral. Full source evidence combines air and water properties with changed-case reasoning; mere recitation of two definitions is insufficient.
