## Four source cards and one environmental account

Write original short cards with the issuer/date/region labels below; provide audio or adult reading where needed. These are paraphrases of public organizational accounts, not invented personal testimony or quotations from an Elder. Teach their distinctions before assessing with unlabelled purpose/concern fields.

1. **Atlantic First Nations Water Authority, its own public Water account.** AFNWA describes water as physically and spiritually important and discusses stewardship from the watershed through water/wastewater service to receiving waters, with cultural knowledge and technical work together. Attribute these views to this First Nations organization and its participant-community remit. Its page contains forward-looking2022operational language: do not treat that sentence as verified2026service status. Learner can describe its stated responsibility/relationship without adopting a belief or taking part in a ceremony. Link: `https://www.afnwa.ca/water/`.
2. **Métis Nation of Ontario, its own Environment & Climate Change account, viewed2026-11-12.** MNO explains a community-based water-quality programme using Métis Guardians and local knowledge with observations and technical/laboratory analysis of water and aquatic ecosystems. Its programme seeks to understand climate/pollution effects on waters important to Métis communities. Use this as a Métis-led monitoring/stewardship perspective; do not claim every citizen performs every test, import its current site counts as permanent facts, or make children sample unsafe water/handle wildlife. Link: `https://www.metisnation.org/programs-and-services/lands-resources-consultations/environment-climate-change/`.
3. **Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, Access to Drinking Water in Inuit Nunangat, Autumn2020, Issue2.** Its introduction/background/infrastructure discussion explains that safe reliable water depends on appropriate maintained community systems, and describes how limited investment and changing conditions can affect access and water quality. Municipal service arrangements and water from the land are distinguished, with land collection outside that report's detailed analysis. Attribute the account and its2020date; it is not a current advisory or a claim that every Inuit household has the same problem or preference. No disease rates, treatment recipe or political knowledge quiz is required. Link: `https://www.itk.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/ITK_Water_English_07.pdf`, introduction/background and infrastructure, printed1–4.
4. **City of Toronto, a named public water-management perspective.** The Wet Weather Flow Master Plan page (modified2026-09-28) explains how rain/meltwater can carry oil, litter, pet waste and other pollutants across hard surfaces to waterways, affecting water quality/habitat; prevention at source and system management are stated aims. Its separate pollution-sources page explains runoff routes and that older combined systems can overflow. Do not say every Toronto drain has the same route or every rain event causes an overflow. Links: `https://www.toronto.ca/services-payments/water-environment/managing-rain-melted-snow/the-citys-wet-weather-flow-master-plan/` and `https://www.toronto.ca/services-payments/water-environment/protecting-improving-lake-river-stream-water-quality/where-water-pollution-comes-from/`.
5. **Health Canada, traffic-related air pollution, page dated2026-02-12.** Its environmental account identifies exhaust, tire/brake wear, road dust and fuel evaporation as pollution sources, and identifies avoiding unnecessary idling and suitable alternative travel as emission-reducing choices. Use only this bounded environmental mechanism/action information; no clinical risk statistics, personal medical advice, bylaw enforcement or driving task is imported. Link: `https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/outdoor-pollution-health/traffic-related.html`.

All selected public bodies/passages were read during preparation. Not all linked reports or the whole ITK paper were reread. Source-specific cultural meanings are attributed, not claimed as laboratory causal findings. Monitoring reveals information and can guide action; monitoring alone does not automatically remove a pollutant. Different organizations can emphasize stewardship, local knowledge/monitoring, reliable infrastructure, habitat, service and daily choices without one being dishonest.

## Teaching comparisons: impact, pathway, perspective and limit

Use original drawings, explicitly labelled models: a road beside a green space; rain running over litter toward a gutter; maintained water/wastewater equipment; and a community monitoring notebook. Draw an arrow only where the supplied facts support a pathway. Learner explains how a human activity can worsen air quality, how a different activity can affect water quality, and how a protective response changes the relevant mechanism. Treat clean-looking air/water as insufficient proof of quality. Water conservation quantity and water-pollution prevention are related but distinct: closing a tap does not remove an existing pollutant.

Compare the actual organizational cards. Ask what each account values or investigates, what evidence/action it emphasizes, and what it does not establish. Include a shared concern and a real difference without inventing antagonism. A supplied fictional driver needing transport and a supplied fictional park user can be added as explicitly imagined needs for weighing feasible actions; they are never presented as interviews, an Indigenous spokesperson or the actual learner's family.

For the complete Different Perspectives and Accounts claim retain its broader changed-account duties: child/grandparent interpretations of an old object; child/driver/shopkeeper views of a public-place change; and how two honest witnesses can differ because of viewpoint/priority. Its complete historical/time/source vocabulary hard path remains separately assessed. The science source comparison does not award all historical inquiry operations. No hard prerequisite is bypassed or weakened for convenience.

For complete Reducing Human Impact, independently identify at least three local negative impacts across land/water/air/living things, propose at least three fitted responses and explain each mechanism. Use the examples of exhaust, litter/runoff and habitat disturbance with suitable responses; a generic recycling slogan does not answer every case. Preserve its complete saved guide and unchanged source flags; it does not become a new water-saving topic.

Record unknown drainage routes or unavailable observations as unknown. The teacher supplies those verified facts; the pupil independently chooses and justifies the plan. ## E1.1 independent plan and changed condition

After teaching, remove completed comparison tables and example plans. Give the genuinely configured locality facts, the separate attributed source cards and a blank plan page. Learner assesses at least one human air impact and one human water impact, takes all three FN/Métis/Inuit perspectives and another relevant view into account, then plans a course of action specifically protecting local air and/or water quality. They need not falsely attribute a plan to any source organization.

The independent plan states its target/pathway, what happens first/next, needed information/materials/permission, who can responsibly act, why the action should protect quality, a feasible check and a limitation. For example, preventing loose litter from reaching a confirmed runoff route targets pollution at source; an adult-authorized non-idling information request targets avoidable exhaust. These are examples, not prefilled pupil answers or guarantees of measured improvement. Gather needed resources before beginning to retain the full Planning a Task operation; a slogan alone is insufficient.

Change a real constraint in the supplied model, such as no permission to alter equipment, a family needing accessible transport, an unknown drain destination, or a water-quality concern persisting despite lower water use. Learner independently revises the plan, keeps essential needs and safety, explains whose concern informed a change, and says what additional evidence would be needed. Correct conservation words alone, a fixed list of actions or a copied adult plan fails this assessment.
