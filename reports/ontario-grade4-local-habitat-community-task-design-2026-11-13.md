## B2.1: necessities in the genuinely local habitat

For a Toronto/HighPark configuration genuinely applicable to the group, describe the actual savannah/woodland area, distinguish the habitat from organisms and use the packet to explain both plant and animal necessities. BlackOak receives light, air, water, nutrients and space from its setting; rodents use acorns for food and suitable trees/dreys/cover as shelter, while still requiring water/air. A resource supplied in the habitat can vary by season/condition; the presence of an animal does not prove every need is adequate.

Pupil independently makes a needs→habitat-feature explanation for a plant and an animal, using source evidence rather than a generic list. Compare BlackOak's dry-upland account with RedOak's moister-slope preference; infer neither that plants need no water nor that every ravine tree must be RedOak. Give a changed light/space/water condition and ask which need/relationship would be affected and what further observation would be needed. The source permits an account of local provision; a performed field observation is only claimed when actual.

For another locality, preserve Toronto as an attributed comparison and substitute a verified actual habitat packet: named plant and animal examples, their actual necessities/provision, reliable dates/source roles and supported conditions. A safe supervised field observation can supplement reliable local records, but a fictional habitat or an unbound adult assertion is not an own-local substitute. No personal address, identity or fieldtrip is required to author the contract. Record unresolved identification/season/access as a delivery condition; never guarantee an organism's presence.

## B2.2: community and factors affecting survival

Teach that a community consists of interacting species sharing a habitat; water/light/soil are important nonliving supports, not species in the community. Use the named oak/rodent/bird examples to explain feeding and shelter, predation and a possible seed-dispersal relationship, not merely a list. An interaction can benefit one participant and harm another; every organism does not help every other one.

After teaching, pupil independently draws and explains at least one plant→animal and one animal→animal relationship, plus living→nonliving need connections. Ask how reduced acorn supply, loss of safe shelter, crowding/limited light or changed water availability could affect both plants and animals. Require a supported conditional chain and a stated unknown, not “everything dies” or a fixed population maximum. Increasing water alone cannot guarantee shelter/light/food. Restore a resource in a fresh hypothetical case and explain why recovery is not instantaneous or certain.

Supply the complete previously authored wetland/dry-grassland models as genuinely fictional transfer cases, retaining all given facts and changed constraints. Compare what can be concluded from the model versus the dated HighPark records; genericW/A labels do not become actual local species. Keep actual source data distinct from a teacher's conditional prediction.
